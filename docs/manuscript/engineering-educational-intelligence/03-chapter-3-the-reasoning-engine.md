# Chapter Three: The Reasoning Engine

## 3.1 Why Representation Is Not Enough

Daniel's record, as the previous chapter left it, is about as complete as a representation can be. His fraction competency is confidence-banded, not merely scored. The decline traces, through the curriculum graph, to an unresolved gap in multiplication fluency, several levels removed from where the symptom first appeared. Every claim behind that trace can be inspected back to the specific evidence that produced it, and none of it overstates what the evidence actually supports. If the argument of the previous two chapters has been followed correctly, this is as honest a picture of Daniel as a system built on evidence, rather than marks, can produce.

It still does not answer the question a teacher actually has standing in front of him: what should happen next?

This is worth pausing on, because it would be easy to assume that a good enough representation eventually answers this on its own — that if the trace is precise enough, the right action simply falls out of it. It does not, and the reason is structural, not a matter of the representation being insufficiently detailed. Knowing where a learner stands and deciding what to do about it are different kinds of claims, resting on different kinds of justification. That Daniel's multiplication fluency is Established-band weak is a claim about evidence: it can be checked, traced, and would be agreed on by anyone who looked at the same evidence log. That he should be given a specific set of remediation exercises, rather than referred for a different kind of support, or simply given more time, is a claim about a *response* — and response is never uniquely determined by evidence alone. Two teachers, looking at the identical trace, could reasonably propose different next steps, not because one of them has misread the evidence, but because deciding what to do involves weighing considerations the evidence itself does not settle: how much instructional time is available, what has already been tried, what else is happening in this particular classroom this particular week.

This is the same distinction, arrived at from the opposite direction, that separated evidence from interpretation in Chapter One and separated a projection's ownership from its consumers in Chapter Two. A representation answers *what is the case*. A decision answers *what should be done*, and a decision requires reasoning — a distinct kind of work, built on top of a trustworthy representation but not reducible to it, and not entitled to the same kind of certainty a well-built representation can claim.

This has an immediate architectural consequence, and it follows the same boundary discipline the previous chapter already established. The Reasoning context, named but left unbuilt in Chapter Two, does not get to reach backward and edit a projection to make its own conclusions more convenient — that would violate projection ownership exactly as directly as the Assessment context writing into the Learner context's evidence log would have violated the boundary that prevented it. Reasoning consumes what representation has already, honestly, produced. It does not get to renegotiate it.

Nor is everything Chapter Two already built now up for reinvention. The capability profile and risk model are mechanical constructs — a trajectory read against a curriculum graph, combined with threshold rules, computed once and reused everywhere, deliberately kept free of any judgment about *why* a pattern exists. That restraint was correct for what representation is responsible for. It is also precisely what makes reasoning necessary as a further, separate layer: a risk model can say, correctly, that Daniel's trajectory has crossed a concerning threshold. It cannot say, on its own, whether that risk is best explained by the unresolved prerequisite gap, by something happening outside the classroom, or by something wrong with how he is being assessed — and a response chosen without knowing which of those is closer to true is a response aimed, at best, by accident.

So the question this chapter exists to answer is not whether a system may reason about a learner — Chapters One and Two have already made clear that intelligence, in the sense this book uses the word, requires exactly that. The question is what reasoning is actually entitled to do, given a representation this careful, and what it is never entitled to do, no matter how useful the shortcut would be.

## 3.2 Educational Claims

Reasoning has to produce something, and it is worth being precise about what that something is, because the word most readily available — "conclusion" — invites exactly the kind of overreach the previous two chapters have spent their length guarding against. Call what reasoning produces an **educational claim**: a specific, traceable assertion about a learner, built from evidence and projections already in hand, that goes beyond what any single projection states on its own, but that remains, in every respect, subject to the same discipline evidence and projections were already held to.

A knowledge gap is an educational claim — not the bare node Chapter Two's traversal mechanically locates, which representation already provides, but the further assertion that this specific gap explains what is being observed, held only as strongly as that explanation is actually earned and never assumed simply because the traversal found somewhere to point. A capability trend is a claim in the same sense — not the trajectory direction Chapter Two's projection already computes, but a judgment of what that direction means against the learner's curriculum position, such as a decline being concerning specifically because it crosses a prerequisite boundary, rather than merely being a decline. So is an elevated risk that names *what kind* of risk, rather than only how urgent — not "Daniel is at risk," which the risk model already says, but "Daniel is at risk specifically because of an unresolved prerequisite, not primarily because of disengagement," which requires reasoning the risk model itself was never built to perform. A particular strength, a suspected misconception, a judgment of readiness to advance, and — reached only at the end of this chapter — a recommendation candidate are all claims of the same kind, differing in subject, not in status.

It is worth being exact about what separates a claim from the two things it sits between. Evidence is what was directly observed — a specific score, on a specific day, from a specific source. A claim is never itself observed; it is built from evidence, and from projections built from evidence, through an explicit act of interpretation, and it must say what that act of interpretation was, not merely assert its conclusion. This is the same boundary Chapter One drew between an observation and the mark it is tempting to treat as equivalent to it, now applied one layer higher: a claim is not a mark either, however confidently it is stated.

On the other side, a claim is not yet an action. "Daniel has an unresolved multiplication gap" describes something about Daniel. It does not, by itself, cause anything to happen to him. This distinction will matter a great deal by the end of this chapter, when recommendations — a specific kind of claim — meet the boundary past which a human, not the reasoning process, decides what actually happens next. For now, it is enough to hold the claim and the action apart: reasoning's entire output, however many claims it produces, stops short of acting.

Naming these types matters for a reason beyond tidiness. A reasoning process that is free to invent new categories of assertion on the fly — to describe Daniel's situation however seems most natural in the moment — cannot be checked for consistency, because there is no fixed vocabulary against which two different claims about two different learners could be compared, audited, or held to the same standard. A fixed, small set of claim types, each with the same underlying discipline, is what makes it possible to ask, of any claim the system has ever produced: was this one built correctly?

That question — what makes a claim built correctly, as opposed to merely stated confidently — is the one this chapter turns to next.

## 3.3 Rules of Educational Reasoning

A claim is built correctly when it obeys a small number of rules, each of which is not new to this chapter so much as an application, to a new kind of object, of something already established.

The first rule is the most basic and the easiest to state: a claim must derive from evidence already in the log, or from projections already built from that evidence, and from nothing else. Reasoning does not get to assert a fact about Daniel that no evidence supports, however plausible it might seem — a system that fills gaps in the record with plausible-sounding inference has stopped reasoning and started guessing, and the difference between the two is exactly the difference this book has been at pains to draw since its opening pages, between a claim earned from what is known and a claim asserted because it would be convenient to believe.

The second rule follows directly from the Confidence Non-Invention Principle, and it is worth stating as its own consequence rather than leaving the reader to infer it: a claim built from one or more projections can never carry a stronger confidence band than the weakest projection it depends on. If Daniel's multiplication-gap claim rests on an Established-band projection at the fraction node and only an Emerging-band projection at the multiplication node — because evidence at that deeper level is thinner — the claim as a whole is Emerging, not Established. Reasoning does not get to average its inputs, round up, or treat a strong link and a weak link in the same chain as though the chain were only as strong as its strongest part. A chain is only as strong as its weakest part, and a claim that depends on several projections inherits the worst confidence among them, not the best.

The third rule ties a claim to the structure Chapter Two built specifically to make this possible: a claim about a competency gap must respect the curriculum graph's actual relationships, not assert a cause the graph does not support. A claim that Daniel's fraction weakness is caused by a gap in a competency with no prerequisite relationship to fractions at all is not a bolder hypothesis — it is an unsupported one, and the graph exists precisely so that this distinction can be checked mechanically rather than argued about.

The fourth and fifth rules are less about how a claim is built than about what happens to it afterward, and they matter because a claim that cannot be inspected or corrected is no more trustworthy than a mark that cannot be traced back to an observation. Every claim must remain traceable — the Evidence Continuity Invariant applies to a claim exactly as it applies to a projection, meaning the chain from the claim back through the projections and evidence that produced it must remain inspectable, not merely technically reconstructible. And every claim must remain revisable: reasoning is never asked to produce a final verdict, only the best-supported claim the current evidence permits, explicitly open to being narrowed, strengthened, or withdrawn as new evidence arrives through the same log that produced the evidence behind it in the first place.

Together, these five rules answer the question the previous section left open — what separates a correctly built claim from a merely confident one — with an answer that has nothing to do with how the claim is phrased and everything to do with what produced it and how honestly it reflects what that production process actually supports.

There is a complication these rules do not yet resolve, and it is not a flaw in the rules; it is a feature of the domain they are meant to govern. Daniel's declining fractions score is evidence the rules above can build several different, individually well-supported claims from — not because the rules are loose, but because the evidence genuinely does not, on its own, pick out one cause over the others.

## 3.4 Competing Explanations

Consider what the evidence actually permits reasoning to conclude about why Daniel is declining, rather than only that he is declining. The multiplication-fluency gap the curriculum graph traversal surfaced is one live possibility, and a well-supported one. It is not the only one consistent with what the evidence log contains. Daniel's attendance record, if the system tracks it, might show an unexplained gap of absences beginning around the same week his scores started falling — a disruption unrelated to any prerequisite. The assessment instrument itself, if its own validity has never been checked carefully at this grade level, might be producing systematically misleading results — this is exactly the possibility the Instrument Validity Gate exists to catch, and no amount of additional corroboration from the same flawed instrument would ever surface it on its own. Or the decline might trace not to Daniel's understanding at all, but to a mismatch between how the material has been taught recently and how he learns best — a claim about instruction, not about Daniel.

Four explanations, each one a claim the rules of the previous section would accept as correctly built, given the evidence available, and none of them ruled out by anything the system currently knows. The naive response is to pick the most likely one — the multiplication gap, say, since it is the one the curriculum graph traversal surfaces most directly — act on it, and move on. This is a mistake, and it is worth being precise about why, because the instinct to collapse to a single answer is strong and the cost of resisting it is real: it takes longer, and it produces a system that recommends nothing until it has more information, in a situation where a confident wrong answer would have felt more useful in the moment.

The mistake is this: choosing one explanation and discarding the others does not make the discarded explanations less true. It only makes them invisible to whatever happens next. If the real cause of Daniel's decline turns out to be attendance, and the system has already committed to the multiplication-gap explanation and recommended remediation exercises accordingly, the intervention that follows will not merely fail to help — it will consume time and attention that a correct response would have spent differently, while the actual cause continues unaddressed. This is the same lesson Chapter One drew from the observation that two identical patterns of falling scores can have opposite correct responses. It reappears here in a sharper form: a reasoning process that resolves ambiguity by force, rather than by evidence, has reintroduced the exact failure this book opened by rejecting, now one layer deeper than a single mutable score, inside the reasoning meant to prevent it.

The correct discipline is to let more than one explanation remain live at once, each held as its own claim, each independently banded according to how much evidence actually supports it, rather than forced into a single ranking that implies more certainty than exists. This is not a probabilistic model in the technical sense — there is no need to compute a numeric likelihood for each explanation, and doing so would import exactly the false precision the Educational Confidence Model was built to avoid. It requires something more modest and more honest: reasoning keeps a small, explicit set of competing claims about the same situation, bands each one according to its own evidentiary support under the rules just established, and does not collapse the set until evidence — not convenience — removes an option. If a check of the assessment instrument's validity comes back clean, the assessment-artifact explanation weakens or drops out. If attendance records show no gap, that explanation drops out too. What remains, narrowed by evidence rather than by impatience, is a smaller set of live explanations that the system is honestly entitled to hold — sometimes one, often more than one, and there is nothing wrong with a system that says, plainly, that more than one explanation remains equally well supported and more evidence is needed to distinguish them.

This is a different kind of output than anything the previous two chapters produced, and it is worth naming precisely what kind: not a single answer, but a disciplined, bounded set of live hypotheses, each one an educational claim in its own right, each independently traceable, each capable of being strengthened, weakened, or retired as new evidence arrives. Reasoning that manages hypotheses this way has not failed to reach a conclusion. It has reached exactly the conclusion the evidence supports — which is, honestly, sometimes more than one.

What remains open is what a live, evidence-supported hypothesis is actually good for. Naming Daniel's likely causes precisely, and refusing to pretend more certainty exists than does, is real progress over a system that would have simply flagged him as declining. It still has not produced anything a teacher could act on tomorrow morning. That is the next and final capability reasoning needs to earn.

## 3.5 Recommendation Generation

A live hypothesis, however well supported, describes Daniel. It does not yet propose anything to do about him. The last kind of claim this chapter needs — the one named without being built, twice, in the previous chapter — is the **recommendation**: a claim that proposes a specific response, tied explicitly to one or more of the claims that justify it.

Everything the rules of Section 3.3 require of a claim applies to a recommendation without exception, because a recommendation is a claim, not a different category of thing that happens to look like one. It must derive from claims already established, not from a judgment reasoning introduces on its own authority. It must respect confidence banding exactly as before: a recommendation built from an Emerging-band hypothesis is itself no stronger than Emerging, regardless of how clearly beneficial the proposed response would be if the hypothesis turned out to be correct. It must remain traceable back through the hypothesis, through the projections, to the evidence — a teacher asking why the system suggests multiplication remediation must be able to follow that chain to its end, the same as a teacher asking why the system flagged Daniel as declining in the first place. And it must remain revisable: a recommendation is a proposal given the evidence available now, not a verdict, and new evidence — including evidence that a previous recommendation did not help — can and should change it.

Given several live hypotheses, reasoning is not obligated to produce a single recommendation, and it would be dishonest to force one where the hypotheses themselves have not yet been narrowed. What Daniel's case actually supports, at this point in the reasoning, is a small set of recommendation candidates, one per live hypothesis: remediate multiplication fluency, tied to the prerequisite-gap hypothesis; look into recent attendance, tied to the disruption hypothesis; review the assessment instrument's calibration at this grade level, tied to the artifact hypothesis. Each candidate carries the band of the hypothesis it depends on, not a band reasoning assigns because one candidate looks more actionable than the others. A recommendation that would be easy to act on is not thereby more likely to be correct, and letting ease of action inflate confidence is only the Confidence Non-Invention Principle's violation wearing a more practical-sounding disguise.

This is, deliberately, less tidy than a system that outputs one suggestion per learner. It is also the honest output of everything built so far, and the alternative — forcing a single confident recommendation out of genuinely unresolved evidence — is not tidiness. It is the same overreach this book has been naming under different names since its first chapter, arrived at now by the most sophisticated path available: not by editing a score, not by inventing a confidence percentage, but by letting a recommendation claim more certainty than the hypothesis underneath it actually earned.

A recommendation built correctly is still, at this point, only words in a log somewhere — a claim about what would help, however well earned. Nothing has actually happened to Daniel yet, and it is worth asking plainly what is allowed to happen next, and who is allowed to make it happen.

## 3.6 Intervention

A recommendation, however well built, is still only a claim about what would help. It is worth stating plainly what reasoning is not entitled to do next, because the temptation to let it do so is real, and the architecture built across this book has been shaped, from the first chapter onward, to prevent exactly this: reasoning does not decide. It recommends, and stops.

Three distinct things are easy to blur together here, and keeping them separate is not a bureaucratic nicety — it is the same discipline that has governed every layer of this book, applied to the boundary where a system's output finally touches a person's actual experience. A **recommendation** is reasoning's output: a claim, banded and traceable, proposing a response. A **decision** is what a human does with that recommendation — a teacher, weighing the recommendation against everything reasoning was never given (the rest of the classroom, what has already been tried, judgment no evidence log could capture) and choosing what will actually happen. An **intervention** is that decision, carried out — multiplication remediation actually assigned, an attendance conversation actually had, an assessment actually reviewed.

Reasoning produces the first of these three and no further. The second belongs to a person, and it belongs there for a reason this book has already earned rather than asserted: a recommendation, however well justified, is built from what the system happens to know, and a teacher's decision is accountable for the whole of what actually happens to Daniel, including everything the system was never positioned to see. Collapsing the recommendation and the decision into one step — letting the system's output become the action directly — would not make the system more capable. It would quietly relocate accountability for Daniel's outcome onto a process that was only ever built to be as good as the evidence and reasoning behind it, and was never built, and is not being built by this chapter, to carry the weight of deciding what happens to a specific child in a specific classroom.

The third of these — the intervention itself — belongs to the world, not to the system at all. Whether multiplication remediation was actually delivered, and whether Daniel's evidence changes afterward, is not something reasoning can determine by further reasoning. It can only be found out.

## 3.7 Closing the Loop

Which is where this chapter's argument returns to something the previous chapter had already named but could not yet fully build: the loop that makes this book's entire architecture worth the word *intelligence*, rather than merely *representation*, closes through exactly this act of finding out.

Every stage of that loop can now be named without leaving anything undefined. Learning happens, unobserved directly, in Daniel. Evidence is recorded about him, gated by instrument validity before it is trusted. Evidence becomes a projection — a trajectory, a confidence-banded competency picture — computed once, owned by the Learner context, never hand-edited. A projection, read against the curriculum graph, becomes reasoning's raw material. Reasoning turns that material into educational claims, holds competing explanations open rather than forcing a premature answer, and — once evidence has narrowed those explanations enough to justify it — produces recommendations, each inheriting, never exceeding, the confidence of what it rests on. A teacher, accountable in a way no part of this architecture is or should be, decides. An intervention happens, or does not, in the world outside anything this book has built.

And then — this is the step Chapter Two could describe only in outline, because nothing had yet been built to fill it — someone observes what happened after the intervention. Did Daniel's multiplication fluency actually improve? Did the attendance conversation reveal something that explains the pattern, or rule it out? That observation is not a new kind of thing this architecture has to invent a special mechanism for. It is evidence, exactly as defined in Chapter One, entered through exactly the same log, subject to exactly the same instrument validity check, banded by exactly the same rules, capable of superseding what came before through exactly the same mechanism a mis-scored quiz would use. Nothing new had to be built to close this loop. It closes because everything built to handle the first piece of evidence in this book was already sufficient to handle the last.

This is what separates the system this book has been building from a system that only ever reports. A reporting system can represent Daniel honestly, reason about him carefully, and recommend something sensible, and still never find out whether any of it was right — every stage up to intervention can be built flawlessly and the system would still, without this final closure, be permanently guessing, more articulately than a mutable grade field guesses, but guessing nonetheless. What makes the loop worth closing is not that it adds one more feature. It is that closing it is the only way the system can ever discover that a claim it made — about a cause, about a recommendation, about what would help — was wrong, and revise accordingly, which is the one thing a system that only asserts, however carefully, can never do for itself.

Everything in this chapter has been built under a constraint stated at its opening and never relaxed: a reasoning process that must remain correct, in the sense of never claiming more than its evidence supports; explainable, in the sense of never producing a claim whose trace back to evidence is not genuinely inspectable; and accountable, in the sense of stopping, deliberately, at the boundary past which a human bears responsibility for what happens next. Holding all three at once, for one learner, in one worked example, has already required real discipline — competing hypotheses held open rather than collapsed, confidence bands that shrink rather than grow as claims compose, a recommendation withheld until the evidence, not the calendar, justified one.

None of this has yet been asked to happen anywhere near the scale at which it will actually need to run — not for one learner considered carefully by a patient author, but for every learner in a classroom, a school, a system, simultaneously, continuously, correctly, without the rules this chapter derived quietly loosening the moment volume makes them inconvenient to enforce. A rule that holds for Daniel, reasoned about by hand, and a rule that holds when the same reasoning must be produced, correctly, for ten thousand learners a day, are not automatically the same rule. What kind of system can actually perform reasoning this disciplined, reliably, at that scale, without the discipline becoming the first casualty of scale itself, is the question this book turns to next.
