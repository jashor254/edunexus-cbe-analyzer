# Chapter Two: The Architecture of Educational Intelligence

## 2.1 Domain-Driven Design for Learning Systems

Suppose the argument of the previous chapter is accepted without reservation, and a team sets out to build it: a system that records evidence rather than scores, computes trajectories rather than averages, and reports confidence honestly rather than asserting it. Suppose, further, that the team does what most teams do — builds one application, with one database, and grows it as requirements arrive. Assessment scoring is added first. A teacher dashboard follows. Then a parent-facing summary. Then, eventually, something that flags learners who might need attention.

This system will work, for a while, and then it will start failing in a specific and instructive way. The code that scores an assessment and the code that renders a parent's summary will both need to know what "evidence" means, and over months of separate changes made by different people under different pressures, their understanding of that word will quietly drift apart. A field added to support the risk-flagging feature will get reused, for convenience, by the report-card feature, and the two will begin to mean subtly different things by the same name. A change to how a trajectory is computed, made to fix a bug in the teacher dashboard, will silently alter what the parent app displays, because both were, without anyone deciding this on purpose, reading from the same internal representation and assuming it meant the same thing to both of them.

This is not a failure of discipline on the part of the team. It is a structural consequence of not having decided, in advance, which part of the system is authoritative for which kind of truth. Chapter One already established that a learner's record must serve several distinct audiences — a teacher, a parent, an administrator — each with a legitimate but different need. It also established that an observation and the interpretation built from it are different things, produced at different times, by different reasoning. Put these two requirements together, and a conclusion follows that has nothing to do with taste: a system that keeps evidence, curriculum, assessment delivery, and interpretation for different audiences all inside one shared, undifferentiated set of tables and functions has no way to keep them from bleeding into each other, because nothing in its structure says they are supposed to be different things.

The fix is not a coding convention. It is a decision about ownership, made explicit and enforced: each kind of truth in the system belongs to exactly one part of the system, and every other part that needs it must ask, rather than reach in and take it. This idea is not original to educational software — it is the central discipline of what software architects call domain-driven design, and the reasoning transfers here directly, because the problem it was built to solve (a domain rich enough that no single, flat model can represent it honestly) is exactly the problem this chapter has just described. Borrowed correctly, it gives a name to what educational systems need: a small number of **bounded contexts**, each with its own vocabulary, its own rules, and its own authority over a specific slice of the truth.

For the system Chapter One requires, the reasoning above resolves into six such contexts. A **Curriculum** context owns the specification of what should be learned — not content, but the competencies and their relationships, which the next section takes up directly. A **Learner** context owns the accumulated evidence about a specific person and the projections derived from it — the trajectory, the confidence, everything Chapter One argued a record must contain. An **Assessment** context owns the design and delivery of instruments that produce evidence, and is deliberately kept distinct from the Learner context that receives it, for the same reason a witness and the detective who interviews them are kept conceptually distinct: one produces an account, the other interprets it, and conflating the two roles makes it impossible to ask, later, whether a mistaken conclusion came from a flawed account or a flawed interpretation. An **Instruction** context owns how teaching is planned against the curriculum. A **Reasoning** context — built in the next chapter, only named here — owns the derived judgments made from a learner's projected state: recommendation, and eventually, intervention. A **Stakeholder** context owns how all of this is presented to a teacher, a parent, an administrator, or anyone else with a legitimate reason to look, and owns nothing else — it does not compute a trajectory, it translates one that has already been computed elsewhere.

The discipline that makes this more than an organizational chart is a single, strict rule: a context may read from another context's published output, but it may never reach into another context's internal representation and treat it as its own. The Reasoning context may consume a Learner context's projection. It may not query the Learner context's raw evidence table directly and compute its own competing version of that projection, because the moment it does, there are two computations of the same fact, and Chapter One has already shown what happens once two computations of the same fact are allowed to exist: they will eventually disagree, and no one will be able to say which one was wrong, because both will have been produced by code that looked correct in isolation.

This rule sounds like a constraint, and it is one, but it is worth noticing what it buys back. Once evidence, curriculum, delivery, interpretation, and presentation are separated into contexts with enforced boundaries, a change inside one of them — a new assessment format, a revised curriculum version, a redesigned parent dashboard — can no longer silently corrupt the others, because there is no path by which it could. The boundary that felt like friction while building the first version of the system is the same boundary that keeps the tenth version of the system from becoming unmaintainable.

None of this, on its own, explains how evidence recorded in one context reliably becomes a trajectory a different context can trust. Drawing a boundary around the Learner context does not yet say what happens the moment new evidence arrives, or how a projection stays correct as evidence accumulates without becoming, itself, a second thing someone could edit by hand. That is the next problem, and it is where the boundaries just drawn either hold under real engineering pressure or quietly dissolve.

## 2.2 The Evidence Pipeline: Ingestion to Projection

Imagine the Reasoning context, built as described above, needing to know whether Daniel's fraction scores are declining. The naive approach is direct: whenever Reasoning needs an answer, it queries the Learner context's evidence store, runs its own trend calculation over the results, and returns an answer. This appears to respect the boundary from the previous section — Reasoning is reading Learner's data, not writing it — but it has quietly recreated the exact problem the boundary was meant to prevent. If two different parts of the system each run their own trend calculation over the same evidence, at different times, using slightly different windowing logic because they were written by different people in different months, they can produce different answers to the same question about the same learner. The boundary held. The coupling did not.

The fix is to make the direction of dependency explicit and one-way. Evidence is appended, once, to an ordered, permanent record — this is the immutable ledger Chapter One already required, now given its architectural name: an **evidence log**. The Learner context is the sole writer of this log. The Assessment context, consistent with the boundary rule established in the previous section, never writes into it directly; it publishes what it observed, and it is the Learner context's own code that turns a published observation into a permanent entry. Nothing downstream of the log is permitted to be a second source of truth about what was observed. Instead, anything that needs a computed answer — a trajectory, a confidence band, an overall competency picture — is built as a **projection**: a derived representation, recomputed from the log whenever new evidence arrives, and never hand-edited directly. If a projection is ever wrong, the fix is never to edit the projection. It is to correct the evidence that produced it, through the supersession mechanism Chapter One already established, and let the projection recompute.

This separation between an append-only record of what happened and a set of recomputable views built from it is not a novelty invented for this book. It is the same structural choice made, for closely related reasons, by systems that cannot afford to lose track of their own history — financial ledgers, audit logs, and any system where the question "what did we believe, and when" has to be answerable long after the fact. The reasoning transfers cleanly here because the underlying problem is the same one: a mutable record of current state can always be edited into a plausible-looking present, at the cost of making the past unrecoverable. An append-only record of what happened, paired with views that are always derived and always re-derivable, gives up nothing and buys back exactly what Chapter One's argument about silent overwrites demanded.

Concretely, a single evidence record needs to carry enough to be interpreted correctly on its own, without a second query back to whatever produced it:

```
EvidenceRecord {
  id: UUID
  learner_id: UUID
  competency_id: UUID
  source_type: SourceType        // e.g. standardized_assessment,
                                  // teacher_observation, ai_scored_exercise
  source_id: UUID                // the specific instrument, teacher,
                                  // or process that produced this
  observed_value: Value
  occurred_at: Timestamp         // when the thing being observed happened
  recorded_at: Timestamp         // when this record entered the system
  supersedes: UUID | null        // set at creation, if this record corrects
                                  // an earlier one; never modified afterward
  dispute_flag: Boolean          // set at creation only, if raised at the
                                  // time of recording; never modified afterward
}
```

Every field on this record is set once, at the moment of creation, and none is ever modified afterward — including `supersedes`. This means a record's standing as current or superseded is never stored as a mutable flag on the record itself; it is a derived fact, computed by checking whether any other record in the log declares `supersedes` pointing back to this one. Storing that status directly on the record and updating it when a correction arrives would be a small, easily overlooked mutation of something this chapter has just finished calling immutable. Deriving it instead keeps the guarantee absolute: nothing about an evidence record, once written, is ever changed — not its content, and not even its own relationship to later corrections.

The distinction between `occurred_at` and `recorded_at` is not a minor detail. A paper assessment marked over a long weekend and entered into the system three days later did not happen three days late; treating the entry date as the observation date would make every piece of delayed-entry evidence look artificially fresh or artificially stale, depending on which date got used where, and a system serving schools without continuous connectivity — which is to say, a great many real schools — cannot afford to conflate the two.

What makes this an architecture rather than a convention is the rule governing how projections respond to the log: every new evidence record is an event, and every context with a projection that depends on the affected competency must recompute, not patch, its view.

```
on EvidenceRecorded(record):
    affected_projections = find_projections(record.learner_id, record.competency_id)
    for projection in affected_projections:
        projection.recompute(evidence_log.for(record.learner_id, record.competency_id))
```

Recompute, here, describes a guarantee, not necessarily a literal execution strategy. What the architecture requires is that a full recomputation from the evidence log remains possible at any time and is treated as authoritative whenever it is performed. A production system may reasonably optimize this with incremental updates, provided the incremental path is periodically reconciled against a full recomputation and any divergence between the two is treated as a defect, not a rounding error. What the architecture forbids is a projection that has quietly become impossible to reconstruct from the log — one whose incremental history is the only remaining account of how it reached its current value. A projection like that can drift from what a full recomputation would produce, unnoticed for years, until the two versions are compared directly and no longer agree — which is precisely the two-truths failure this whole chapter exists to prevent, now showing up not between two features, but between a projection and its own history.

This gives the Learner context a trajectory it can trust and a confidence-bearing picture that recomputes correctly as evidence arrives or is corrected. It does not yet say what that picture is *of*. A trajectory needs a reference to be judged against — some notion of what a learner at this point should already know, and what any given weak spot depends on. Without that reference, a projection can say a learner is declining, but it cannot say declining *in what*, in a way precise enough to act on, and Chapter One's second requirement — that a system be able to say something about cause, not only pattern — remains unmet.

## 2.3 Knowledge Graphs and Curriculum Structure

Return to Daniel's decline once more, now armed with a trustworthy, recomputable projection of his performance in "fractions." The projection can say, correctly, that his competency in this area is falling. It cannot yet say why, and a teacher acting on "falling in fractions" alone is in almost the same position as a teacher acting on a bare average: informed that something is wrong, with no leverage on what to actually do differently tomorrow.

The reason the projection cannot say more is that "fractions" has been treated, so far, as a single, undifferentiated label — one node with no relationships to anything else. Real competencies are not like this. Adding fractions with unlike denominators depends on being able to find a common denominator, which depends on fluency with multiplication, which was, in most curricula, taught years earlier and never revisited once considered mastered. If Daniel's actual weakness is an old, unaddressed gap in multiplication facts, then every fraction-specific exercise assigned to him is aimed at the wrong target — technically about the right subject, but at the wrong depth, treating a downstream symptom as though it were the disease.

A flat list of competencies, however completely it covers a curriculum, cannot represent this kind of dependency, because a list has no relationships, only membership. What the curriculum actually needs is a structure that can express that one competency depends on another — a directed graph, where nodes are competencies and edges carry an explicit relationship: prerequisite-of, reinforces, applies-in. A tree would almost work, and is tempting because it is simpler to build and easier to display, but a tree forces every competency to have exactly one parent, and real prerequisite relationships do not respect that constraint — multiplication fluency is a prerequisite for fraction arithmetic, for scaling recipes in a science unit, and for reading certain kinds of charts in a social studies unit, three different strands with one shared foundation a tree cannot represent without either duplicating the node or arbitrarily picking one parent and hiding the others.

With the graph in place, gap analysis stops being a guess and becomes a traversal:

```
Algorithm: locate the root cause of a competency gap

input: learner's projection, curriculum graph
output: the deepest unresolved prerequisite behind an observed gap

find the competency node C where the learner's projected level
    is below the expected level for their grade

for each ancestor A of C, walking backward along prerequisite edges:
    if the learner's projected level at A is also below expectation:
        continue walking backward from A
    otherwise:
        stop — A is mastered; the gap does not originate here

return the deepest node reached where the learner's level first
    falls below expectation
```

Run against Daniel's evidence, this traversal does not stop at "fractions." It walks backward through "finding a common denominator," finds that projection also low, continues to "multiplication fluency," finds the actual origin of the decline three levels removed from where the symptom first appeared. The redirection this makes possible — toward multiplication fluency, not fraction procedure — points to a different and better target than anything a flat competency list could have produced, and it is a direct, mechanical consequence of representing the curriculum as a graph rather than a list. Nothing about this required guessing at Daniel's motivation or state of mind; it required only that the structure of the curriculum be represented as faithfully as the structure of his evidence.

Two honest limits are worth naming rather than glossing over. Some domains — narrow, largely independent skills, of the kind found in short compliance-training modules — have little real prerequisite structure to represent, and a graph with no edges is simply the correct, if unremarkable, representation for them; nothing is lost by allowing that degenerate case. And some learners require a curriculum graph that departs from the standard one — a student on an individualized program may have a personally adapted structure of prerequisites, and the architecture needs to support a learner-specific graph as a first-class case, not treat it as an exception to be handled outside the system.

The curriculum graph answers what a projection is a projection *of*. It does not yet answer a question that has been quietly deferred since the end of the previous chapter: how much should any single node's projected level actually be trusted, especially when the evidence behind it is thin, contradictory, or drawn from an assessment whose own quality has never been checked? That question can no longer be postponed.

## 2.4 The Educational Confidence Model

Chapter One left this problem stated but unsolved on purpose: a single piece of evidence and twenty corroborating pieces are not different by degree, and collapsing that difference into a single decimal number creates an appearance of precision the underlying situation does not have. The problem can now be solved properly, because the pieces it requires — an evidence log, recomputable projections, a structured curriculum to project onto — are already in place.

The solution is not a number. It is a small, fixed set of states, each with criteria precise enough to compute and coarse enough to be honest about what is actually known. Call this structure, in full, the **Educational Confidence Model**. Every claim the system makes about a learner — a competency level, a trajectory direction, eventually a recommendation — occupies exactly one of four **confidence bands**.

A claim resting on a single observation, with nothing yet corroborating it, is **Provisional**. A claim with two or more observations, or one high-reliability observation, within the relevant recency window, but not yet corroborated by an independent source, is **Emerging**. A claim supported by at least three observations from at least two independent source types, within the recency window, with no unresolved contradiction, is **Established**. A claim that meets the Established criteria and has remained stable across more than one recency window — surviving, in effect, a second look — is **Confirmed**.

One consequence of this structure deserves to be stated as a rule rather than left implicit, because it is the exact point at which systems most often quietly cheat: a learner with a single piece of evidence has a Provisional claim, and a Provisional claim has no trend. Not a stable one — none at all. A single point defines no direction, and a projection that reports "stable" for a brand-new learner because "stable" sounds safer than "we don't know yet" has smuggled a false claim in through a default value. The correct behavior is to say, plainly, that there is not yet enough evidence to characterize a trend, and to keep saying so until a second, independent observation exists to compare against the first.

Four inputs determine which band a claim earns, and each one closes a specific gap the argument so far has left open.

**Source reliability** ranks the type of instrument that produced an observation — a standardized, validated assessment outranks an informal note, which outranks an unverified automated signal — fixed in advance for each source type, never assigned by whoever happens to be entering data that day.

**Recency** is computed from `occurred_at`, never from `recorded_at`, for the reason established in the previous section: evidence does not become fresher because it was typed in promptly, and a system that confuses the two will misjudge exactly the learners whose evidence arrives late through no fault of their own. Each competency type carries its own validity window — a fractions assessment ages differently than a career-interest signal — declared once, not assumed universal.

**Corroboration** asks whether independent sources agree, and this is where most naive implementations fail without realizing it. Two observations corroborate each other only if they differ in at least one of: the individual who produced them, the institutional role that individual held, or the instrument used. A teacher's written observation and that same teacher's own quiz do not corroborate one another — they are one judgment, recorded twice, and a system that counts them as independent agreement has manufactured confidence it has not earned. Corroboration, correctly defined, is the mechanism that does the real epistemic work in this model; the other three inputs qualify evidence, but only corroboration turns a pile of qualified evidence into something a system is entitled to trust more than any single piece of it.

**Volume** — the raw count of evidence — is deliberately the weakest of the four. Ten uncorroborated, low-reliability observations should not outrank one corroborated, high-reliability one, and a model that let volume substitute for corroboration would simply relocate the illusion Chapter One opened with: instead of an average masking a trajectory, a count would be masking an absence of real agreement.

When these inputs conflict, the order in which they are applied matters, and it is worth making explicit rather than leaving to whichever engineer happens to implement it first. Recency is applied as a filter, before anything else: evidence outside its validity window is excluded from the computation entirely, not merely down-weighted. Corroboration and volume are then computed only over what survives that filter. This is what allows the model to handle a learner whose ability changes quickly — after intensive tutoring, after an injury, after any real change in circumstance — without a large body of old, thick, but now-irrelevant evidence outvoting a small amount of new evidence simply because there is more of it. Old evidence does not get out-argued. It ages out, and only what remains gets a vote.

None of this is worth building if the resulting band can be quietly overridden downstream. The rule that closes this off, stated as plainly as the rest of this model deserves to be stated, is the **Confidence Non-Invention Principle**: no layer of the system may assign a claim a band higher than its own inputs justify, and no consumer of a claim may raise the band it receives. A reasoning process — built in the next chapter — that consumes an Established projection may produce a recommendation from it, but that recommendation does not thereby become Confirmed simply because it was expressed persuasively. This applies as much to language as to data. A claim at Provisional band, described in prose as "clearly" or "definitely" true, violates this principle exactly as surely as a claim with an inflated numeric score would, because the reader receives more certainty than the underlying evidence licenses — the certainty has simply moved from the number into the word choice. Any system, including one that uses AI to generate explanations of its own claims, must map bands to language honestly: Provisional and Emerging claims are described with visible hedges; only Established and Confirmed claims are asserted without qualification.

A new learner, with no history at all, presents one more case this model has to handle honestly rather than conveniently. It is tempting to give such a learner a starting projection based on how similar learners have typically performed — and there is nothing wrong with using that information, as long as it is never allowed to masquerade as evidence about the individual it is attached to. A claim built from population statistics rather than personal observation is permitted, but only as an explicitly and permanently labeled non-individual category, capped at Provisional regardless of how much population data supports it, and never blended into the learner's own evidence as though a classroom of other students' history were something this particular learner had done.

Bands are not permanent, and this matters as much as how they are earned. Each competency's recency window defines a horizon; once the most recent corroborating evidence ages past it, the band steps down — Confirmed to Established, Established to Emerging, and so on — until refreshed by new evidence. A model that computes confidence once and lets it sit is not meaningfully different from a mutable `current_grade` field that happens to be phrased more carefully; both go stale silently unless decay is built in as a standing behavior, not a maintenance task someone remembers to run.

For prose and for schemas alike, a claim's confidence is written as `Band[claim]` — `Established[capability: fraction_addition]`, for instance — or, where a structure is needed, as a record:

```
ConfidenceClaim {
  band: Provisional | Emerging | Established | Confirmed
  source_reliability: Reliability
  recency: RecencyWindow
  corroboration: CorroborationResult
  volume: Integer
  last_refreshed: Timestamp
}
```

There is one more gap this model, as built so far, cannot close on its own, and it is worth taking seriously precisely because everything above it is constructed correctly. Every input just described — reliability, recency, corroboration, volume — assumes that the evidence being weighed is a fair reflection of what it claims to measure. Suppose it is not. Suppose the assessment used to generate Daniel's fraction scores is itself badly constructed — ambiguously worded, miscalibrated, testing something other than what it claims to. No amount of corroboration rescues this, because corroboration only confirms that independent sources *agree*, and a flawed instrument, used repeatedly, will produce evidence that agrees with itself convincingly and is wrong regardless.

This requires a check that happens *before* confidence is computed at all, not alongside it: an **Instrument Validity Gate**. An instrument's validity — whether it actually measures what it claims to — is itself a claim, evidenced by validation studies and independent replication, and there is no need to invent a second scoring system for it; validity is banded using exactly the same four bands and four inputs already built, applied one level up, to the instrument rather than to the learner. The binding rule follows directly: confidence may only be computed for evidence produced by an instrument holding Established validity or higher. Evidence from an unvalidated or under-validated instrument is still recorded — nothing is ever discarded, in keeping with the immutability this book opened by requiring — but it is excluded from confidence-banded claims and marked, explicitly, as provisional pending validation, never silently blended in as though it carried the same weight as evidence from an instrument that has earned trust.

The same mechanism applies one level further still, to the curriculum graph itself. A prerequisite edge — the claim that mastering one competency depends on another — is also a claim, capable of being wrong, and capable of being shown wrong by later research even when no one entered any incorrect data. An edge below Established validity should not be treated as load-bearing for gap detection; the traversal in the previous section walks past it, but does not build a confident recommendation on top of it, until the edge itself has earned the trust the rest of this model requires of everything else.

With evidence, projection, confidence, and instrument validity all in place, the shape of the whole system becomes visible for the first time as a single, continuous process rather than a sequence of separately motivated fixes. Learning itself is never directly observed — evidence is the only access to it, gated by instrument validity before it is trusted at all. Evidence becomes a projection, confidence-banded at every step, never asserted beyond what its inputs justify. A projection, in the next chapter, becomes the input to reasoning, and reasoning becomes a recommendation, and a recommendation becomes an intervention someone is accountable for carrying out. What closes the system, and what turns it from a pipeline into something that deserves to be called intelligence rather than reporting, is that the outcome of that intervention is itself observed, and folded back in as new evidence — never overwriting what came before, only extending the record, exactly as this chapter's evidence log was built to allow.

This closing step is worth dwelling on for a moment, because it is easy to build a system that skips it and still looks complete. If the record of what happened after a recommendation were derived from the recommendation itself — if the system simply assumed its own advice had been followed and worked — it would be validating itself against its own output, which is not verification at all, only an elaborate way of hearing an echo and mistaking it for a second opinion. The loop has to close through what actually happened in the world — through observation, not through the system's own prior confidence — or nothing about it can be trusted to have learned anything. This full cycle — evidence, projection, reasoning, recommendation, intervention, observation, feeding back into evidence — is what this book calls the **Educational Intelligence Loop**, and the name is meant literally: it is the loop, not any single stage of it, that earns the word *intelligence*. A system that never closes it can still generate scores, dashboards, and alerts, competently and indefinitely. What it cannot do is discover it was wrong. That distinction is the one this book uses, from here forward, to separate a reporting system from an educational intelligence system, and it is not a matter of sophistication — a very simple system that closes this loop belongs on one side of that line, and a very sophisticated one that does not belongs on the other.

What makes any of this trustworthy enough to act on is a property that can be checked directly, at any moment, against the system's own data, independent of watching any particular computation run. For every claim the system holds — a band, a projection, a recommendation once the next chapter builds that far — there must exist an unbroken, inspectable path back to the specific evidence that justifies it, where inspectable means a reader could actually follow it, not merely that a query could technically return every record ever touched; a trace of unbounded size that no one could review in practice does not satisfy this any more than no trace at all would. And every piece of evidence at the end of that path must still hold current instrument validity at the moment the trace is checked, not merely at the moment it was recorded — because an instrument invalidated after the fact should pull every claim built on it back into question, not leave them standing on a foundation that has since been withdrawn. This guarantee has a name — the **Evidence Continuity Invariant** — and it is not a new mechanism bolted onto everything built so far. It is the single sentence that describes what all of it, done correctly, was already guaranteeing.

What remains is a question this chapter has so far only answered one competency at a time. Daniel's fraction gap, its root cause, and the confidence behind both can now be stated precisely — but only for fractions, one node at a time. Whether that precision holds together into something a teacher, a parent, or an administrator could actually use is the next problem.

## 2.5 Capability and Risk Models

A curriculum graph with confidence-banded projections at every node gives the system everything it needs to answer detailed, competency-specific questions about a learner. It does not, on its own, answer the broader questions a teacher, a parent, or an administrator actually asks: how is this learner doing overall, and are they at risk of falling behind in a way that needs attention now rather than later?

The tempting shortcut is to let each feature that needs an answer compute its own summary. A teacher dashboard rolls up competency bands into a class overview one way; a risk-alerting feature scans for declining trajectories another way; a term report aggregates for a different audience with different framing. Each of these, built independently, will look reasonable in isolation and will not, in general, agree with each other, because "how is this learner doing overall" does not have one obvious answer, and three independently written implementations will resolve the ambiguity three different ways. The result is the same failure this chapter opened by naming: two — here, three — computations of the same underlying fact, quietly capable of disagreeing, with no way to say which one is right, because all three were built correctly according to their own separate logic.

The architectural response is not clever aggregation. It is refusal to duplicate the computation at all. A learner's **capability profile** — the full set of confidence-banded competency levels across the curriculum graph — is computed once, in the Learner context, and every feature that needs a summary of it consumes that single computation rather than rebuilding an approximation of its own. A **risk model** is built the same way: not as a second, independent judgment about the learner, but as a specific reading of the same capability profile, combined with trajectory direction and threshold rules, still traceable back to the identical evidence every other view of this learner is built from. If a teacher dashboard, a risk alert, and a parent report can ever disagree about the same underlying fact, the defect is not in any one of them — it is in the decision to let three features compute a shared fact three times. This is worth holding as a general rule, because it recurs constantly in systems of this kind: whenever two features can disagree about the same fact, the system has two truths, not two features, and the fix is always to consolidate the computation, never to reconcile the disagreement after the fact.

## 2.6 Explainability as an Enforceable Contract

A consolidated capability profile and risk model solve the disagreement problem. They do not, by themselves, solve a different one: a teacher looking at a risk flag, or a parent reading a report, is entitled to ask why the system believes what it claims to believe, and a system that cannot answer — that can only repeat the claim more emphatically — has not actually produced intelligence in the sense this book has been building toward. It has produced an assertion, and an assertion a reader cannot inspect is not meaningfully different from a guess dressed in confident language, whatever computation happened to produce it.

The right response is not a feature added at the end, translating internal state into a human-readable explanation after the fact. Everything necessary for a real explanation has already been built, by construction, into every layer described so far: every projection recomputes from specific evidence records; every confidence band is derived from named inputs, not asserted; every trace back through the curriculum graph passes through specific, identifiable nodes. Explainability, done this way, is not a UI problem to be solved with better copywriting. It is the Evidence Continuity Invariant, made visible to the person asking the question — the same guarantee that already holds internally, surfaced rather than invented. A system built on the architecture described in this chapter can answer "why does it say this" honestly, because the answer was never hidden inside the computation; it was the computation, all along, and the only remaining work is to present it — hedged exactly as its band requires, per the language-register rule already established — to the person who asked.

## 2.7 Chapter Synthesis: The Reference Architecture

Put together, the pieces of this chapter form a single structure, not a list of independent decisions. Bounded contexts keep evidence, curriculum, delivery, interpretation, and presentation from bleeding into each other. An evidence log, appended to once and never overwritten, feeds recomputable projections rather than a second, editable source of truth. A curriculum graph gives those projections something precise to be projections of, and turns "something is wrong" into "this specific prerequisite is unresolved." The Educational Confidence Model turns accumulated evidence into honest, bounded claims, gated by instrument validity so that a flawed measuring instrument cannot manufacture false agreement, and governed throughout by a single rule against inventing certainty that has not been earned. A capability profile and risk model consolidate all of this into one authoritative picture per learner, computed once, so that no two features can honestly disagree about the same fact. And the whole structure is inspectable end to end, by design, because nothing in it was ever permitted to discard the path back to the evidence that justified it.

None of this, on its own, decides what should happen next for a learner like Daniel. It produces a trustworthy account of where he stands and how sure the system is entitled to be about that — nothing more. Knowing that his fraction gap traces to an unresolved multiplication weakness is not the same as knowing what a teacher should actually do about it, this week, with thirty-nine other students in the room. That is a different kind of question. It requires reasoning about causes and options, not merely representing evidence correctly, and it requires deciding what a system is entitled to recommend, as distinct from what it is entitled to know. That is where this book turns next.
