# Production #001 Table Read — "Why ¾ Is Bigger Than ⅗"

**Status:** First formal read-aloud review of the [existing script](edunexus-production-001-script-lab.md). No line has been changed. This document identifies problems and assigns editorial decisions only — the next pass is a separate, explicit editing step, not this one.

**Script under review, for reference, unchanged:**

> Two fractions: three quarters, and three fifths. Which is bigger? *[pause]* Most people say three fifths — five is bigger than four, so it feels like more. That's not careless. It's the same rule that's worked for every whole number you've ever compared. Let's look. One whole, cut into four pieces. Three shaded: three quarters. The same whole, cut into five pieces. Three shaded: three fifths. *[pause]* Three quarters is bigger. Five is bigger than four. So how can three fifths be smaller? *[pause]* Look at what changed. Same whole, same three pieces taken — only the number of cuts is different. Four cuts, bigger pieces. Five cuts, smaller pieces. The denominator isn't how much you have. It's how finely the whole was divided. *[pause]* More cuts to the same whole make each piece smaller. *[pause]* Try it: two thirds, or two sevenths. Same numerator — which whole was cut more finely? *[pause]* Two thirds. Fewer cuts, bigger pieces. Next time you share something evenly, cut it two ways — and see it for yourself.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

The script survives its first table read intact in structure but not unmarked. Nothing found below requires reopening the Cognitive Journey, the mechanism, or the chosen example — the room's shared verdict is that the bones are sound. What the read surfaced is smaller and more specific: two sentences quietly pack two new ideas into one breath at exactly the moments the Pattern Library says load should be lowest; one line of reassurance ("that's not careless") risks introducing the very self-doubt it's trying to head off, a well-known hazard of negated framing; and — the room's most consequential finding — the script's shortest, most punchy line ("Three quarters is bigger," line 8) is a genuine competitor for 24-hour recall against the line actually meant to survive ("More cuts to the same whole make each piece smaller," line 13). A learner could walk away remembering the right fact and the wrong lesson.

None of this is fatal. All of it is fixable in a single, targeted editorial pass, not a rewrite. **Verdict: READY AFTER EDITORIAL PASS.**

---

## 2. First Listening Experience

*(As if hearing it once, cold, with no prior context — reactions pooled from the Grade 7 Learner, Struggling Learner, and Parent perspectives.)*

**What remains:** "Three quarters is bigger" — vivid, short, delivered right after the visual beat, with a pause landing right before it for emphasis. Also a rough shape of "more cuts, smaller pieces," though not necessarily in the script's exact words.

**What is forgotten:** the specific transfer numbers (two thirds, two sevenths) tend to blur — they arrive late, close together, after the piece has already delivered its main payload, and a first-time listener's attention has measurably eased off by that point.

**What confused, on first pass:** a small but real stumble around "three fifths — five is bigger than four" (line 3) and again "Five is bigger than four... three fifths" (line 9) — the near-rhyme of *five* and *fifths*, spoken twice, close together, caused a brief re-listen in two of three test reads (Struggling Learner and Parent personas both asked for a repeat at this exact point; the High-achieving Learner did not).

**What immediately made sense:** the paired "one whole, cut into four... the same whole, cut into five" lines (6–7), read back to back — the room agreed this is the clearest, most well-built pair of sentences in the entire script, and nobody hesitated on it.

Walking the three learner personas through each major beat.

| Beat | Struggling Learner | Average Learner | High-achieving Learner |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Which is bigger?" | Guesses fast, low confidence | Guesses fast, moderate confidence | Guesses fast, may already sense a trick is coming |
| "Most people say three fifths — five is bigger..." | Relief — "that's what I said" | Same, mild recognition | Slight impatience — already suspects this is a setup |
| "Let's look" / the two wholes | Full attention — this is the clearest moment for this persona | Full attention | Attention, but already predicting the answer before the visual finishes |

## 4. Speaking Audit

Read aloud, sentence by sentence, for rhythm and breath — not content.

- **Line 3** ("Most people say three fifths — five is bigger than four, so it feels like more.") — reads fine on the page, less clean aloud: the em-dash joins two distinct clauses into one breath, and a reader has to choose whether to breathe mid-sentence (breaking the flow) or push through (running long). **Sounds slightly written, not spoken.**
- **Lines 6–7** (the paired "one whole... the same whole...") — deliberately parallel construction, and it reads beautifully aloud; the room had no notes here. Flagged only to note the *intentional* repeated structure is working as designed, not a flaw.
- **Line 9** ("Five is bigger than four. So how can three fifths be smaller?") — nearly repeats line 3's construction ("five is bigger than four"). This repeated structure is deliberate (echoing the prediction to sharpen the contradiction) but combined with the five/fifths proximity flagged in Section 2, this is the single spot in the script most likely to trip a reader or momentarily confuse a listener.
- **Line 10** ("Look at what changed. Same whole, same three pieces taken — only the number of cuts is different.") — another em-dash-joined double clause, same issue as line 3: reads as compressed writing rather than natural speech.
- **Line 11** ("Four cuts, bigger pieces. Five cuts, smaller pieces.") — short, clipped, fragment-style. This is unusual for spoken narration generally, but works here — it reads like a memorable, punchy soundbite rather than an awkward fragment. No note.
- **Line 12** ("...how finely the whole was divided.") — "finely" is the one word in the script a reader might stumble very slightly on for emphasis placement, but it is not a tongue-twister and not flagged as a genuine problem.
- **Line 14** ("Try it: two thirds, or two sevenths. Same numerator — which whole was cut more finely?") — a third instance of the same em-dash-compression pattern found in lines 3 and 10. By this point in the read, the pattern is recognizable as a habit across the script, not three isolated choices.
- **Places where silence communicates better than words:** the room agreed the pause after lines 6–7 (before "Three quarters is bigger") is the single strongest silent beat in the piece — nobody wanted narration filling that gap. No line in the script currently over-narrates a moment that should be silent instead.

**Pattern-level finding:** three separate sentences (3, 10, 14) share the same em-dash-compression habit, joining a setup clause and a payload clause in one breath. This is a genuine, recurring structural tic worth naming as one finding, not three unrelated ones.

## 5. Cognitive Audit

Checking for sentences that ask the learner to process two new ideas at once, per Part 4's instruction.

- **Line 3** — packs *what* the common wrong answer is ("three fifths") and *why* it feels right ("five is bigger than four... feels like more") into a single sentence. Two distinct cognitive contents, one breath. **Flagged for split.**
- **Line 10** — packs the instruction to look ("look at what changed"), the specific constant ("same whole, same three pieces"), and the specific variable ("only the number of cuts is different") into one sentence — three components, technically, though the last two are closely related enough to possibly count as one. **Flagged for split**, erring toward giving the "what's constant" and "what varies" halves separate breaths, since this is the Investigation stage specifically, the one place the Pattern Library allows Medium load but no more.
- **Line 14** — packs the new example ("two thirds, or two sevenths"), the reminder of the operative concept ("same numerator"), and the actual question ("which whole was cut more finely") into one breath, arriving right at the point in the piece (Section 2, Section 3) where the Struggling Learner persona already shows signs of pacing strain. **Flagged for split**, and flagged as the highest-priority of the three given where it lands in the piece.
- **No other line** in the script was found to pack two new ideas simultaneously — lines 4, 6–9, 11–13, 15–16 each introduce exactly one new element, consistent with the Playbook's own scripting discipline.

---

## 6. Emotional Audit

- **Line 4** ("That's not careless.") — this is the one line the room debated longest. The intent (per the Creative Development review) is to reassure the viewer their wrong prediction was reasonable, not sloppy. But naming the concept being denied — "careless" — can, for a self-conscious learner, plant the very word the line is trying to keep away. A viewer who wasn't thinking "was that careless of me?" may now briefly be. This is a subtle, well-documented risk of negated reassurance generally, not specific to this script, but real here. **Flagged for tightening**, without a proposed replacement (per this document's own constraint against rewriting).
- **No other line** produced a judged, embarrassed, lectured, rushed, ignored, or overwhelmed reaction from any of the three learner personas in Section 3 — the script's consistent use of "most people," never "you," when describing the wrong prediction is doing real, effective protective work throughout, and the room specifically credited this choice as working exactly as the House Voice Standard's non-judgment principle requires.
- **Line 9**'s question ("So how can three fifths be smaller?") was checked specifically for a "gotcha" or quizzing tone and cleared — it reads as shared curiosity, not a challenge aimed at the viewer, because it never resolves into "you were wrong," only into "here's what's actually going on."

## 7. Teacher Review

**Where a teacher would say "that's exactly the misconception":** the entire Expectation/Prediction sequence (lines 1–4) and the mechanism statement (lines 11–12) — the veteran Mathematics Teacher persona and the CBC Curriculum Specialist persona both independently confirmed this is a faithful, recognizable rendering of how learners actually reason, not a strawman.

**Where a teacher would push back, "no learner actually thinks like that":** nowhere in the core misconception framing — but the Curriculum Specialist persona raised a real, specific concern about **line 4**: "It's the same rule that's worked for every whole number you've ever compared" implicitly frames fraction comparison as entirely new territory for the viewer. But the Creative Development review's own diagnosis (its Section 2, "why it survives normal teaching") establishes that Grade 7 learners typically *have* already been taught a fraction-comparison procedure — they've seen fractions before, just without the underlying concept sticking. Line 4, read literally, slightly undersells that prior exposure, and a sharp CBC teacher could reasonably note the line isn't quite accurate for a learner who's already sat through a common-denominator lesson, even if it's still true that *this specific* rule (denominator size) has never been corrected for them. **This is an internal-consistency finding, not just a nitpick** — it's a small gap between what this script implies about the audience and what the Creative Development review already established about the same audience.

---

## 8. Memory Review

Twenty-four hours later, the room's honest, pooled prediction: a listener most likely retains **"three quarters was bigger than three fifths"** — the specific fact — more reliably than **"more cuts to the same whole make each piece smaller"** — the general rule this entire piece exists to install.

**Why line 8 competes with line 13 for recall, stated plainly:** line 8 ("Three quarters is bigger") is short, concrete, arrives immediately after the strongest visual/silent beat in the script (Section 4), and is the first moment of resolution the viewer experiences — all four of these are, independently, known memorability boosters. Line 13 arrives later, is more abstract, and — critically — doesn't have a comparably strong silent beat isolating it as *the* moment to remember, relative to how strongly line 8's surrounding pause isolates it. **This is the single most important finding of this table read.** It does not mean the script is wrong about what should be remembered — the Creative Development review's Memory Analysis (its Section 8) is correct that line 13 should be the survivor — it means the current staging doesn't yet give line 13 a fair fight against line 8 for that role.

| Pause location | Function |
|---|---|
| After "Which is bigger?" | Creates thought — the room agreed this is essential and, if anything, could run slightly longer to give every learner persona a genuine chance to commit to a guess |

| After "So how can three fifths be smaller?" | Creates thought, with a mild secondary effect of tension — both are appropriate here, no note |
| After "...how finely the whole was divided" (before line 13) | Borderline — by this point the content of lines 11–12 has already delivered the mechanism in concrete form; line 13 mostly *generalizes* what's already been said rather than introducing something new. The pause here may be doing less genuine thinking-work than the earlier ones, since there's less new material to process. **Flagged: pause shorter.** |
| After line 13 (reflection) | Creates thought — correctly timed, matches the Story Engine's requirement that Reflection get real, uninterrupted silence |
| After line 14 (before line 15's answer) | Creates thought — mirrors the first pause well; no note, though see Section 5's finding that line 14 itself may need to be split before this pause can do its job for every learner persona, not just the fastest ones |

**No pause in the script was found to simply waste time or create unintended confusion** — the one flagged pause (before line 13) is a timing-proportion note, not a functional failure.

## 10. Sentence Survival Review

Every sentence, tested by removal, per Part 10's strict rule: if removing it doesn't weaken learning, it shouldn't exist.

Applying this test line by line reproduces the [prior script audit](edunexus-production-001-script-lab.md#4-sentence-by-sentence-educational-audit)'s findings for fifteen of sixteen lines — each was independently re-confirmed as load-bearing in this session's read-through, with no dissent.

**Line 16** ("Next time you share something evenly, cut it two ways — and see it for yourself.") is the one line where this table read reaches a different, more severe conclusion than the earlier script-level audit did. The earlier audit flagged it as "removable without weakening the core claim" but stopped short of recommending removal, citing the Creative Development review's own recommendation to include a parent/practice extension. Applying Part 10's test strictly and without that earlier hedge: the core educational claim (Sections 1–2, and the Memory Test target in Section 6) is fully and completely delivered by line 15. Line 16 adds real value — Section 7 of the Creative Development review is right that it strengthens transfer beyond the screen — but "adds real value" and "learning becomes weaker without it" are different findings, and Part 10 asks specifically for the second. **Honest verdict: by the letter of the cut test, line 16 should not exist.** This directly disagrees with the Creative Development review's recommendation to include it — a genuine, worth-naming tension between two different review passes, not something to quietly resolve in this document. The Editorial Standards Council should be the one to decide which review's framing governs, not this table read.

## 12. Final Verdict

**READY AFTER EDITORIAL PASS.**

Not READY FOR STORYBOARD — five specific lines (3, 4, 9, 10, 14) and one pause carry decisions that should be resolved before visual planning locks pacing against this text, and the line 16 disagreement between this read and the Creative Development review needs an explicit Council ruling, not a default. Not REWRITE BEFORE STORYBOARD — nothing found here touches the Cognitive Journey, the chosen mechanism, the example, or the transfer test; the misconception framing was independently confirmed by both teacher personas as faithful, and the room found no line that judges, embarrasses, or rushes the learner beyond the one narrow, fixable case in line 4.

The script earned its structure. It has not yet earned its final wording — six small, named decisions stand between this draft and one ready to be read against a stopwatch and a storyboard.
