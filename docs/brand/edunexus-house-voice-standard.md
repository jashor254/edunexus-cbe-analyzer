# The EduNexus House Voice Standard

**Status:** Foundational brand asset — research/architecture phase only. No voice has been generated, selected, or synthesized. No TTS technology has been installed or evaluated in production.

**Scope:** This document defines *who the EduNexus narrator is* before any technology decision is made. It is the permanent identity that every future text-to-speech, voice-cloning, or human voice-actor engagement must faithfully reproduce. Technology changes; this document should not.

**Audience for this document:** founder, future voice director, future audio engineer, any voice actor or studio EduNexus ever hires or auditions.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

EduNexus needs one narrator, used everywhere, forever. Not "an AI voice for now until we find something better" — a permanent cast member, the same way a university has a seal or a broadcaster has a house style. The reasoning: EduNexus's entire product thesis is that a learner's record, evidence, and journey are *coherent over time* — one continuous account of a real child's growth, not a pile of disconnected app features. A narrator who changes from video to video undercuts that thesis at the exact moment it should be reinforced. The voice is the sound of "EduNexus is speaking," and it should sound like the same trusted person whether it's explaining a CBC competency to a Grade 8 learner in a TikTok clip or presenting Career Intelligence findings to a Ministry official.

The core finding of this audit: EduNexus does not need a voice that is exciting. It needs a voice that is *believable across a decade, across every audience segment simultaneously, and across every register from a 15-second hook to a 30-minute documentary*. Believability, not charisma, is the design target. A charismatic voice is a liability the moment it becomes dated or the moment a parent's trust depends on sounding like a documentary rather than an advertisement.

The recommendation in brief (expanded fully in Section 14): a warm, unhurried, East African English narrator — Kenyan-grounded but pan-regionally intelligible — who reads like a excellent, calm secondary-school teacher explaining something they genuinely find interesting to someone they respect. Not a broadcaster. Not a salesperson. Not an AI assistant. A teacher who has time for you.

## 2. Why This Voice Exists

**"The narrator exists to make a complicated, high-stakes system feel legible and safe to a parent, teacher, or child who has every reason to distrust an AI education product."**

That is the job. Not to entertain. Not to persuade. Not to demonstrate technical sophistication. EduNexus operates in a space — a child's academic future, in a market where AI is regarded with a mix of hope and suspicion — where the wrong emotional register actively damages the product's credibility no matter how good the underlying intelligence is.

**Role the narrator plays:** a translator between the system's intelligence (evidence, projections, recommendations) and a human's understanding of their own child, student, or classroom. The narrator is never the smartest person in the room; the narrator is the person who makes the smart thing understandable.

**Emotional responsibility carried:** the narrator is often the first and only "human" layer between a family and a claim about their child's ability, risk, or trajectory. That is not a light responsibility. Every sentence needs to sound like it was said by someone who understands the weight of being wrong about a child.

**Relationship with the audience:** trusted, respected near-equal — not an authority figure lecturing down, not a peer being casual, not a service agent being deferential. Closest real-world analogue: a respected Head of Department addressing a staffroom, or a favourite teacher addressing a class they clearly like.

**What the narrator should never become:** a salesperson, a hype machine, a robot pretending to have feelings, or a performer competing for attention. The moment the narrator sounds like it is *trying* to be liked, trust erodes. See Section 9 for the full forbidden list and the reasoning behind each exclusion.

---

## 3. Audience Analysis

The same voice must land, unchanged, with: learners (Grade 7–12, roughly ages 12–18), parents, teachers, school leaders, researchers, government stakeholders, and the general public.

This looks like a contradiction — a child and a Ministry official are not usually served by the same register — until you notice what all seven groups actually share: **none of them want to be talked down to, and all of them are evaluating whether this platform can be trusted with something that matters to them.** A 13-year-old doesn't want a cartoon voice any more than a policy analyst wants jargon. Both want clarity and respect.

The resolution is not to build seven voices or seven scripts-per-audience. It is to hold **one constant delivery** — calm, clear, unhurried, respectful — and vary only *vocabulary and pacing of content*, never *tone of person*. A script for Grade 7 learners uses shorter sentences and concrete examples; a script for a government stakeholder uses precise terminology and data references. The same person is reading both, and a listener moved from one to the other would recognize it as the same narrator without needing to be told.

Practically: the narrator should sound like an adult a 14-year-old would trust *and* an adult a school principal would trust in the same breath — which in practice means avoiding both ends of an axis: never child-coded ("fun uncle/auntie" energy, exclamation-heavy) and never institution-coded (formal, distant, corporate). The centre of that axis is a specific, real archetype in Kenyan schools: the teacher every student secretly prefers because they explain things plainly and never make you feel small for not knowing — and whom parents trust because that same teacher has never once oversold a result.

## 4. Psychological Profile

Design brief, as if hiring a real person for a permanent role.

| Trait | Target | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Age impression** | Early-to-mid 30s | Old enough to carry unforced authority; young enough to still sound curious rather than settled/tired. Not youthful-sounding (undermines authority with government/researcher audiences), not senior-sounding (reads as distant to learners). |
| **Authority** | Earned, not asserted | Authority that comes from clearly understanding the material, never from volume, pace, or vocal weight. Should never need to "sound important." |
| **Warmth** | High, understated | Present in every sentence as a baseline temperature, not performed as an emotional peak. Warmth you'd only notice if it were missing. |
| **Confidence** | Quiet, total | No hedging tics, no upward inflection turning statements into questions. Never needs to convince you it's confident. |
| **Humility** | Present, load-bearing | Comfortable saying "here is what the evidence shows" rather than "here is the truth." Central to trust — see Section 6 (Educational Identity) and the Evidence-First Mandate this platform already operates under. |
| **Curiosity** | Real, restrained | Sounds like someone who still finds the subject interesting after years of explaining it — not manufactured wonder. |
| **Patience** | Very high, structural | Never rushes toward a conclusion. Comfortable with silence and with re-explaining. This is arguably the single most important trait for a *permanent* narrator — impatience is what makes a voice tiring at 30 minutes and dated in 10 years. |
| **Empathy** | Present but not sentimental | Acknowledges difficulty without dramatizing it ("this is a tricky idea" not "I know this must feel overwhelming"). |
| **Professionalism** | High, invisible | Shows up as consistency and precision, not as stiffness. |
| **Calmness** | The dominant trait | Everything else is built on top of calm. A narrator explaining a child's risk profile to a parent cannot, ever, sound alarmed, rushed, or falsely reassuring. |
| **Energy** | Moderate-low, steady | Enough to sustain attention; never spikes for effect. Flat-lines toward "engaged stillness," not toward monotone. |
| **Humour** | Rare, dry, never at anyone's expense | A narrator that never smiles is cold; a narrator that jokes often is unserious. Occasional, light, observational — the kind of humour a good teacher uses to release tension, not to entertain. |

**The balance in one sentence:** *authority without distance, warmth without performance, patience without slowness.*

## 5. Acoustic Profile

| Parameter | Target |
|---|---|
| **Pitch** | Mid register — neither notably low ("movie trailer") nor notably high ("excitable"). A natural, unforced speaking pitch for an adult in their 30s. |
| **Resonance / Depth** | Moderate chest resonance, enough to carry weight in low-volume playback (phone speakers, classroom projectors) without needing bass-heavy audio equipment. |
| **Brightness** | Mid-low. Bright/forward voices read as commercial or youthful; the target sits slightly warmer/darker than a typical ad-read voice, closer to long-form documentary or audiobook brightness. |
| **Breathiness** | Minimal. A small amount of natural breath texture reads as human and unforced; excess breathiness reads as intimate/ASMR-adjacent, which is wrong for an educational-authority context. |
| **Texture** | Smooth, with light natural grain — not glassy/digital-clean (reads synthetic and cold), not rough/gravelly (reads world-weary or aged beyond the target). |
| **Pace** | Genuinely unhurried: roughly 140–155 words per minute for explanatory content (below typical ad-read pace of ~160–180 wpm, above slow meditative pace of ~110–120 wpm). Adjust down slightly for dense/technical passages, adjust up slightly (not more than 10%) for TikTok hooks — never into "excited" territory. |
| **Cadence / Rhythm** | Prose rhythm, not verse rhythm. Natural variance in phrase length. Avoid the sing-song up-down cadence of movie-trailer narration and the flat metronome of a corporate e-learning voice. |
| **Sentence flow** | Ideas complete before the next begins. No run-ons stitched by breathless connectors ("and — and then — and so"). |
| **Pause placement** | Pauses mark thought boundaries, not just punctuation. A pause before a key term or number is used deliberately, sparingly — never as a rhetorical trick ("...and the answer... is remarkable"). |
| **Sentence endings** | Fall in pitch and settle. Statements end like statements. Avoid uptalk (rising terminal pitch), which reads as uncertain or, worse, as trying to sound approachable in a manufactured way. |
| **Emphasis** | Emphasis lands on meaning-bearing words (the new information in a sentence), never on adjectives for effect. One emphasized word per sentence at most in ordinary explanatory content. |
| **Projection** | Conversational-close, not stage-projected. This is a voice speaking *to* one listener through a screen, not addressing an auditorium. |
| **Breathing style** | Audible enough to feel human; positioned at natural phrase boundaries; never gasped or dramatized. |

## 6. Linguistic Profile

- **Sentence length:** short-to-medium as the default (8–18 words); long sentences permitted only when a single connected idea genuinely requires it, and always resolved with a clear subject–verb–object spine so a listener never loses the thread mid-sentence.
- **Reading speed:** see Pace above — 140–155 wpm baseline.
- **Vocabulary:** plain, concrete, Kenyan-English-standard. Prefer "shows," "means," "so far," "based on" over "indicates," "signifies," "to date," "predicated upon." Avoid corporate/ed-tech jargon ("leverage," "unlock," "empower," "journey" used as marketing filler, "solution," "seamless").
- **Clarity:** every sentence should be understandable on first listen with no rewind — this is a non-negotiable design constraint given the platform is often consumed on phones, in classrooms, with background noise.
- **Pronunciation:** standard Kenyan English pronunciation (see Section 7) — never anglicized/Americanized to sound "more neutral," never exaggerated for "authenticity."
- **Technical terminology:** CBC/CBE terms (competency, strand, sub-strand, learning outcome, proficiency level) pronounced with full clarity and unhurried care the first time they appear in any script, since they are frequently the least familiar words to a parent audience.
- **Numbers:** always read in full, natural spoken form ("seventy-eight percent," never "seven eight percent" or a clipped digit-read). Grades and forms read as Kenyan educators say them ("Grade Seven," "Form Three") — never Americanized ("seventh grade").
- **Curriculum language:** KICD terms pronounced as officially rendered; acronyms (CBC, CBE, KICD, KNEC) spoken as letters, evenly paced, never rushed together.
- **Foreign/Swahili words:** where Swahili terms are used intentionally (e.g., "shule," a learner's name, a county name), pronounced correctly and respectfully at natural Kenyan-English cadence — never anglicized, never over-emphasized as a performance of authenticity.
- **Acronyms:** always expanded on first use per script/video, spoken clearly, one beat of separation from surrounding words.

## 7. African English Identity

No stereotyping. No movie-narrator imitation. No exaggerated accent. The target is an authentic, professional East African English — the register already used by Kenyan news anchors, university lecturers, and senior educators on national broadcast — not a performance of "African-ness" for an international audience.

- **How much regional character should remain:** the full, natural amount. Standard Kenyan English rhythm, vowel quality, and intonation patterns should be fully present and unapologetic. This is not a voice reaching toward British or American neutrality — it is confidently, natively East African, the same way a BBC narrator is confidently British without anyone calling that an "accent choice."
- **How neutral the pronunciation should be:** neutral *within* the East African register (broadly intelligible across Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, Rwanda — the platform's plausible expansion geography per the company roadmap) — not neutral toward a Western standard. "Neutral" here means "the register a Kenyan newsreader uses when addressing the whole country," not "the register that erases where the voice is from."
- **What pronunciation should never be lost:** natural Kenyan English vowel and consonant patterns, natural sentence-stress and rhythm patterns distinct from British/American English, correct and unhesitant pronunciation of Kenyan place names, Swahili loan-words, and learner names — this last point matters enormously for a platform that names real children in its output.
- **What makes the voice recognisably African without becoming theatrical:** authenticity comes from *unselfconsciousness* — the voice sounds like this because this is how this person naturally speaks, not because an accent is being demonstrated. The test: would this voice sound identical whether or not anyone was evaluating it for "African authenticity"? If yes, it's right. Theatricality creeps in the moment pronunciation, rhythm, or vocabulary seem selected *for* the listener's benefit rather than simply belonging to the speaker.

## 8. Educational Communication Style

How this differs from adjacent registers, each contrasted directly:

| Register | Core difference from EduNexus narration |
|---|---|
| **News reading** | News is neutral-to-urgent and moves fast because stories compete for attention within a bulletin. EduNexus narration is unhurried because there is no competing story — the listener's attention is already committed. |
| **Advertising** | Advertising creates desire and urgency ("don't miss out"). EduNexus narration creates understanding and calm. No urgency language, no calls to action delivered with excitement. |
| **Motivational speaking** | Motivational registers build emotional peaks and rely on vocal intensity to transfer conviction. EduNexus narration transfers understanding, not conviction — it trusts the evidence to be convincing, not the delivery. |
| **Corporate narration** | Corporate narration is polished toward blandness and optimized to offend no one; it often sounds like it belongs to no one. EduNexus narration should sound like it belongs to a specific, real, warm person. |
| **Podcasts** | Podcast hosting is conversational, digressive, and personality-forward — the host's opinions and reactions are part of the content. EduNexus narration has no opinions of its own; it explains what the evidence shows. |
| **YouTube influencer style** | Built on high energy, reaction, and parasocial performance to hold attention against infinite competing content. EduNexus narration holds attention through clarity and relevance, never through performed enthusiasm. |

**The one-line test:** the narrator should feel like a trusted educator, never an entertainer. If a script reads well as an ad-read, it is wrong for EduNexus, regardless of how good the read is.

## 9. Emotional Range

The narrator has a range, but every point in that range is recognisably the same person — the way a good teacher's voice changes when praising a student versus explaining a difficult concept, while never stopping being obviously the same teacher.

| Moment | How the voice moves | What stays constant |
|---|---|---|
| **Explaining** | Slightly slower, evenly paced, clean pauses before key terms | Calm baseline, no rise in pitch |
| **Encouraging** | Slight warmth increase, softer consonants, unhurried | No exclamation-point energy; encouragement is quiet, not loud |
| **Correcting / flagging a concern** | Slightly slower still, gentler, more space between clauses | Never sharp, never clipped — corrections should sound like care, not judgment |
| **Challenging (asking a learner to think harder)** | Slightly more energy, genuine curiosity texture, real questions (rising only where grammatically a question) | Never confrontational, never rhetorical-trick delivery |
| **Celebrating** | Warmest point in the range, mild upward energy, but still controlled | Never shouts, never performs surprise |
| **Asking questions** | Natural question intonation, unhurried, genuinely inviting a pause for thought | No game-show cadence |
| **Introducing evidence / data** | Measured, precise, slightly more deliberate pacing on numbers | No dramatized reveal ("...and the number is...") |
| **Ending a lesson / video** | Settles, slows very slightly, closes with a falling, complete cadence | Never a cliffhanger trick, never an artificial upsell tone |
| **Revealing an important insight** | A brief, natural pause before the insight; then delivered plainly and clearly | Insight is delivered with clarity, not suspense — the moment earns attention through content, not vocal trickery |

The unifying constraint: **emotional range should be visible on close listening but never obvious in isolation.** A single 5-second clip pulled from anywhere in the emotional range should still sound calm, warm, and credible.

## 10. Forbidden Characteristics

Voices EduNexus must never become, and why each specifically damages trust in this product:

- **Movie trailer** — built entirely on manufactured tension and scale. A child's education is not a spectacle; using trailer cadence trivializes the subject matter and reads as manipulative to any adult listener.
- **Radio advertisement** — optimized to sell in 30 seconds via urgency and hard-sell energy. EduNexus is asking for long-term trust, not an impulse purchase; ad energy actively signals "this is being sold to you," which is the opposite of the desired read for evidence about a child.
- **Salesperson** — persuasive-by-design, closes on emotional pressure. A parent evaluating whether to trust an AI system with their child's academic record should never feel *sold to*.
- **AI assistant (Siri/Alexa-style)** — bright, brisk, transactional, personality-neutral by design so it can serve any request. EduNexus's whole differentiator is that it is *not* a generic assistant — it holds a specific, evidence-grounded relationship with a specific learner. Sounding like a generic assistant undercuts that positioning at the acoustic level.
- **Robotic assistant** — flat, synthetic cadence signals "machine speaking at you," which triggers exactly the distrust of AI-in-education this narrator exists to overcome.
- **Politician** — persuasive cadence built for applause lines and repetition, optimized to be quotable rather than accurate. Reads as evasive the moment nuance is required — and nuance (confidence levels, evidence limitations) is core to how this platform must speak.
- **Preacher** — rhythmic, rising-intensity delivery built to move a crowd emotionally. Wildly inappropriate register for presenting a risk assessment about a 13-year-old.
- **Motivational guru** — performs certainty and energy to inspire action. EduNexus's Evidence-First Mandate requires humility about what is and isn't known; guru-cadence is structurally dishonest for this content.
- **Sports commentator** — built on real-time excitement and pace escalation. Explaining a competency gap is not a match highlight; this register would feel grotesque applied to a child's struggles.
- **News anchor** — authoritative-neutral, optimized for rapid topic-switching across unrelated stories all day. Too brisk and too impersonal for a platform speaking about one specific child's specific evidence.
- **Overly dramatic documentary narrator** (the "In a world..." register) — treats ordinary information as momentous through vocal weight alone. Fine for wildlife footage; corrosive for education, where overstating stakes vocally, rather than letting real stakes speak for themselves, reads as manipulative once noticed — and parents notice.

Every one of these fails for the same underlying reason: each optimizes for *holding or moving an audience's emotions in the moment*, while EduNexus's narrator must optimize for *being trusted over years*. Those two goals pull in opposite directions, and every forbidden register above picks the wrong one.

## 11. The Listening Test

| Duration | Expected listener experience |
|---|---|
| **10 seconds** (TikTok hook) | Should read as clearly, calmly human and immediately credible — not attention-grabbing through energy, but through clarity of the opening idea itself. No fatigue risk at this length; the test here is *trust formed instantly*. |
| **30 seconds** (short explainer) | Warmth should be evident; authority should be evident; no sense of being sold anything. |
| **2 minutes** (typical lesson clip) | The calm pacing should start paying off — listener settles in rather than waiting for something more exciting to happen. Trust should visibly increase over this window. |
| **10 minutes** (teacher training / longer explainer) | This is where most narrator voices reveal fatigue-inducing habits (uptalk, filler cadence, over-bright tone). The target profile — unhurried, mid-register, minimal vocal tics — should show *no* build-up of listener fatigue. Authority should feel fully established by this point. |
| **30 minutes** (documentary / institutional presentation) | The calmness and patience that felt merely pleasant at 30 seconds should now feel like the entire reason the content is bearable at this length. Warmth should not fade — a common failure mode where narrators "flatten" over long-form reads. Authority should be at its peak: a listener who has spent 30 minutes with this voice should feel they know the narrator. |

**Fatigue risk assessment:** low, by design — the acoustic profile deliberately avoids the two biggest long-form fatigue drivers: excess brightness/sibilance and uptalk/rising cadence. The main risk to actively manage in production is monotone drift at pace 140–155 wpm sustained past 15 minutes; mitigate with natural micro-variance in pacing per idea, not per rule.

**Ideal long-form profile in one line:** a voice that gets *more* trustworthy the longer you listen, never less.

## 12. Casting Brief

For any human audition, AI voice-actor licensing evaluation, or synthetic-voice source-selection — the criteria below apply identically.

**Immediate rejection criteria:**
- Any performance energy detectable — reads like they're "doing a voice" rather than speaking
- Uptalk / rising terminal pitch as a habitual pattern
- Bright, forward, ad-read brightness that can't be dialed back
- Accent reaching toward American or British neutrality rather than owning East African English
- Audible strain, rush, or breathlessness at natural reading pace
- Cannot sustain calm, controlled delivery past 5 minutes without energy drop-off or fatigue creeping into the sound
- Any trace of condescension when reading content aimed at children
- Any trace of insincerity or "sales" texture when reading claims about outcomes or evidence

**Shortlist criteria:**
- Genuinely calm, controlled default delivery without being coached toward it
- Correct, natural East African English pronunciation and rhythm
- Demonstrates all nine traits from Section 4 at a baseline level
- Reads technical/curriculum terminology cleanly on first pass
- Comfortable with silence — doesn't rush to fill pauses

**Exceptional (rare) criteria:**
- The listener forgets they are listening to a "voice" and simply follows the meaning — total transparency of delivery
- Emotional range (Section 9) present and audible without ever breaking character
- A 30-second and a 20-minute sample are unmistakably the same calm, unhurried person with zero perceptible fatigue or tone shift
- Sounds equally natural narrating a TikTok hook and a Ministry presentation without the actor needing to "switch modes" — the register itself already sits at the correct centre point
- Correctly and warmly pronounces Kenyan learner names, place names, and Swahili terms without hesitation or over-articulation

This is a casting brief for a *person*, not a prompt for a model — the same criteria apply whether the eventual source is a professional voice actor recorded for cloning, a licensed commercial synthetic voice, or a custom-trained model; the identity comes first, in every case.

## 13. Technology Review — deferred by design

Per this document's mandate, no technology (XTTS, Piper, ElevenLabs, OpenVoice, F5-TTS, StyleTTS2, or any commercial API) is evaluated here. That evaluation is Phase 2 and should not begin until:

1. This House Voice Standard is reviewed and approved as the fixed identity target.
2. A casting process (Section 12) has identified either a real voice-actor source recording (for cloning/fine-tuning approaches) or a small set of reference commercial voices that most closely approximate this profile (for API-based approaches).
3. Reference audio exists — even scratch recordings — against which any technology's output can be judged for fidelity to Sections 4–9, not for general voice quality.

The evaluation question in Phase 2 will not be "which of these sounds best?" It will be: **"which of these can most faithfully reproduce the person defined in this document, across the full listening-test duration range in Section 11, and hold that fidelity for a decade of production use?"**

## 14. Longevity Test

Projected ten years forward:

- **Would this narrator still sound appropriate?** Yes — the profile deliberately avoids every trend-coded marker (no influencer cadence, no current ad-read brightness fads, no meme-adjacent delivery). It is built on the same register that has held up for decades in serious documentary and educational broadcast — a register whose stability *is* the design goal, not a side effect.
- **Would they age well?** Yes — the target age impression (early-to-mid 30s, calm, patient) is itself timeless rather than youth-coded; it will not read as "trying to sound young" in 2036 the way an influencer-adjacent voice would.
- **Would they become tiring?** No — the profile is explicitly optimized against the two failure modes that make long-running brand voices tiring: performed energy and repetitive vocal tics. A calm, varied, unhurried voice is the one register that doesn't wear out its welcome with repetition.
- **Would they become dated?** No more than a genuinely excellent teacher's voice becomes dated. Trend-driven vocal styles date quickly; unforced, calm authority does not have a "style" to date.
- **Would listeners still trust them?** Yes, more so — a voice heard consistently for ten years across thousands of pieces of content, that has never once oversold, exaggerated, or sounded like an advertisement, accumulates trust the way a long-serving, well-regarded teacher does. This is the entire point of fixing the identity now rather than iterating voice-by-voice.

## 15. The House Voice Standard — Definitive Specification

**Narrator Philosophy**
The EduNexus narrator exists to make complex, high-stakes information about a real child feel legible and safe. It is a trusted educator, not an entertainer, salesperson, or assistant. It never competes for attention — it earns it through clarity.

**Voice Personality**
Early-30s impression. Calm above all else. Warm but understated. Quietly, totally confident. Patient to a fault. Genuinely curious about the subject. Humble about what the evidence does and doesn't show. Rare, dry, gentle humour. Never performs any of the above.

**Acoustic Profile**
Mid pitch, moderate warm resonance, mid-low brightness, minimal breathiness, smooth texture with light natural grain. Pace 140–155 wpm baseline. Natural prose cadence, falling sentence endings, no uptalk. Conversational-close projection. Emphasis on meaning, not adjectives.

**Pronunciation Rules**
Authentic, unhesitant standard Kenyan English throughout — never anglicized toward British/American neutrality, never exaggerated for effect. Numbers spoken in full natural form. Grades/forms spoken as Kenyan educators say them. CBC/KICD/curriculum terms and acronyms pronounced fully and clearly on first use per script. Kenyan/Swahili names and place names pronounced correctly and warmly, without hesitation or over-articulation.

**Reading Guidelines**
Short-to-medium sentences as default. Plain, concrete vocabulary; no ed-tech or corporate jargon. Every sentence understandable on first listen, no rewind required. One idea per sentence wherever possible.

**Emotional Guidelines**
A visible but narrow range (Section 9) built around a calm baseline. Every emotional register remains recognisably the same person. No exclamation-point energy anywhere in the range, including celebration.

**Accessibility Guidelines**
Clear articulation of every consonant, especially at sentence-ends, to support listeners using captions alongside audio, low-quality phone speakers, and non-native or hearing-impaired listeners. No rapid-fire delivery anywhere, including hooks. Numbers and technical terms always fully and slowly articulated the first time they appear.

**Quality Standards**
No performance energy detectable. No fatigue or tone drift across a 30-minute sample. Identical perceived "person" across a 10-second clip and a 30-minute clip. Zero instances of ad-read, sales, or influencer cadence in any produced content.

**Forbidden Characteristics**
Movie trailer, radio advertisement, salesperson, generic AI assistant, robotic assistant, politician, preacher, motivational guru, sports commentator, news anchor, overly dramatic documentary narrator — and any hybrid of these. (Full reasoning in Section 10.)

**Approval Criteria**
A candidate voice — human or synthetic — is approved only if it passes the full Listening Test (Section 11) at every duration, meets every Casting Brief shortlist criterion (Section 12) without exception, and a blind listener cannot detect which of two same-script clips (one at 10 seconds, one at 20 minutes) is the "warmed up" version — because there should be no warmed-up version; the voice should arrive at full trust from the first sentence.

---

## Final Recommendation

Do not select a technology yet. Commission or identify a small set of real reference recordings — ideally a working Kenyan voice actor, or if unavailable, a small set of existing commercial voice samples — evaluated strictly against Sections 4 through 12 of this document, not against general voice-quality impressions. Use those references to write a one-page casting brief external evaluators (a studio, a voice-actor marketplace, or a TTS vendor) can be handed directly. Only once reference audio exists that a founder, a teacher, and a parent can all independently listen to and say "yes, that is the EduNexus voice" should Phase 2 (Section 13, technology comparison) begin.

This document is the fixed point. Every future production — TikTok, YouTube, parent education, teacher training, documentary, Career Intelligence, product launch, research or conference presentation, institutional video — inherits this identity unchanged. The voice does not evolve production-by-production. The technology reproducing it may.
