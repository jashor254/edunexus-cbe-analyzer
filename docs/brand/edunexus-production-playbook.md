# The EduNexus Production Playbook

**Status:** Operational handbook — workflow and review discipline, not architecture. No script, no scene, no Manim code, no artwork. This document assumes the [House Voice Standard](edunexus-house-voice-standard.md), [Visual Language Standard](edunexus-visual-language-standard.md), [Pattern Library](edunexus-pattern-library.md), and [Story Engine](edunexus-story-engine.md) are complete and settled. It answers a different question than any of them: **given all four already exist, how does one creator, on one Tuesday, actually use them to take an idea to a publishable piece without drifting from any of them?**

**What this document is:** the workflow — the sequence of questions a creator asks themselves, the checklists they run, and the gates a piece must clear before it advances, from first idea to post-publication review.

**What this document is not:** a restatement of the four Standards it applies. Where this Playbook references "voice," "visual," "pattern," or "story" quality, it points back to the governing document rather than re-deriving the rule — this Playbook's job is sequencing and gatekeeping, not re-authoring policy.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Four excellent standards do not, by themselves, guarantee four consistent productions — they guarantee four consistent standards *available to be ignored under deadline pressure*. The gap this Playbook closes is the one every studio eventually discovers the hard way: the difference between having a style guide and actually following it every single time, especially on the piece nobody expects to matter, made in a hurry, by whoever's available.

This Playbook is built around a simple operating principle: **no piece advances to the next stage of production until it has explicitly passed a checklist derived from the Standard that stage concerns.** Not "we generally follow the House Voice Standard" — a specific checklist, run against a specific piece, with a pass/fail outcome, at a specific gate, before rendering ever begins. The Playbook's real deliverable is not new creative guidance — the four prior documents already provide that — it is *discipline*: the workflow that makes drift from those standards a visible, catchable event rather than a slow, invisible erosion.

The second load-bearing idea in this document is that **quality is measured after publication, not just before it.** A piece that passes every pre-publication gate can still fail to produce real understanding in real learners — the only way to know is to look, deliberately, at what happened after release, and feed that back into the next production cycle. A studio that only measures pre-publication compliance will produce technically correct pieces that don't actually teach; Section 10 exists specifically to prevent that blind spot.

## 2. Production Philosophy

The mindset every creator holds *before* opening any tool, derived directly from what's already been established across the Voice, Visual, Pattern, and Story documents:

**Questions every creator must ask before beginning work:**

- What is the one specific thing a viewer should understand after this that they didn't before? (If this can't be answered in one sentence, the idea isn't ready — see Section 4.)
- Who, specifically, is this for — which of the platform's real audiences (Pattern Library, Section 3 of the Visual Language Standard) is this piece's actual primary viewer?
- What evidence exists to support every claim this piece will make? (Not "what evidence could I find" — what evidence already exists, per the Evidence-First Mandate this whole platform operates under.)
- Which Canonical Story Structure (Story Engine §11) does this idea actually fit? If none fit cleanly, is this genuinely a new structure, or is the idea not yet a story?
- What would this piece look like if I removed everything that wasn't strictly necessary to the one sentence above?

**Assumptions every creator must avoid:**

- That excitement, novelty, or visual polish can substitute for evidence or clarity — every prior Standard in this series explicitly rejects this trade.
- That "this is a small piece, the full process is overkill" — the failure modes in Section 12 disproportionately originate in exactly these pieces, precisely because they get less scrutiny.
- That a pattern, metaphor, or visual device not yet in the Pattern Library or Visual Dictionary can be improvised for one piece and formalized later — informal use precedes and normalizes drift; anything genuinely new goes through the admission process (Pattern Library §11) before use, not after.
- That the creator's own intuition about what's "engaging" overrides what the Standards specify — intuition is a useful signal to raise at review, never a substitute for review.

**The responsibility every creator carries:** this platform speaks, in the aggregate, about real children's evidenced record. A creator working on a Career Intelligence explainer or a Blueprint presentation animation is not making marketing content — they are making a claim, in the studio's permanent voice, about how a real learner should be understood. That responsibility does not scale down for a 15-second piece.

## 3. Workflow

The full pipeline, stage by stage, with the governing document for each stage noted. Gates (Section 9) sit between stages; nothing below moves forward without clearing its gate.

```
1. Idea Intake           → Section 4 (Story Preparation)
        ↓  [Gate 1: Idea Gate]
2. Pattern Selection      → Section 5 (Pattern Selection Guide)     [uses Pattern Library]
        ↓  [Gate 2: Pattern Gate]
3. Story Design           → Story Engine's ten-stage Lifecycle
        ↓  [Gate 3: Story Gate]
4. Scripting               → (outside this Playbook's scope — governed by Story Engine + House Voice)
        ↓  [Gate 4: Voice Gate]      → Section 6 (Voice Checklist)
5. Visual Planning         → (outside this Playbook's scope — governed by Visual Language Standard)
        ↓  [Gate 5: Visual Gate]     → Section 7 (Visual Checklist)
6. Learning Review          → Section 8 (Learning Checklist)
        ↓  [Gate 6: Learning Gate]
7. Accessibility Review     → Section 9 (Accessibility Checklist)
        ↓  [Gate 7: Accessibility Gate]
8. Production & Rendering   → (Production/Rendering Pipeline — outside this Playbook's scope)
        ↓  [Gate 8: Editorial Standards Council Final Sign-off]
9. Publication               → (Publishing Pipeline — outside this Playbook's scope)
        ↓
10. Post-Publication Review   → Section 10
        ↓
11. Feedback into future Idea Intake (loop closes)
```

Two things about this workflow are non-negotiable. First, gates are ordered deliberately — Voice and Visual review only happen once Story Design has settled the Lifecycle and Pattern Selection has settled the cognitive content, because reviewing tone or motion against a story structure that's still shifting wastes the reviewer's judgment on a moving target. Second, the loop closes: Section 10's findings are a required input to the next Idea Intake, not a report that gets filed and forgotten — a Playbook without that closing loop teaches a studio to keep making the same mistake with increasingly polished production values.

## 4. Story Preparation — From Idea to Story

Before a single pattern is chosen or a single sentence of script is written, an idea has to earn its way into being a story candidate.

**Identifying the real learning objective:** ask "what should a viewer be able to do or understand after this that they couldn't before?" — per the Story Engine's ladder (§2), if the honest answer is "know a fact," the idea is currently Information, not yet a story, and needs to be pushed further before proceeding. Write the objective as one sentence, in plain language, before anything else. If it can't be written as one sentence, the idea is still two or more ideas tangled together — split them.

**Removing unnecessary complexity:** list every fact, qualifier, and sub-point the idea currently seems to require, then ask, for each: "does removing this change whether the learning objective is met?" If not, cut it. This is a direct, practical application of the Pattern Library's coherence principle (§3) at the idea stage, before it has cost anything to produce — cutting here is nearly free; cutting after scripting or animation is expensive, which is exactly why this step happens first.

**Discovering the central question:** the learning objective (stated above) usually implies a question the story should be built to answer — state it explicitly, in the form a real member of the target audience would actually ask it ("why did my child's risk flag appear?" not "an explanation of risk methodology"). This question becomes the Story Engine's stage 2 (Question) directly; if a natural, concrete question can't be found, the idea may be better suited to a reference document than a story (see Section 12's "false story" failure mode).

**Output of this stage:** one sentence (the learning objective), one question (in the audience's own words), and a first guess at which Canonical Story Structure (Story Engine §11) fits — carried forward into Gate 1.

## 5. Pattern Selection Guide

Using the Pattern Library's 100 canonical patterns and its own Cognitive Load Matrix and Combination Matrix directly.

**Choosing the primary pattern:** ask which of the twelve categories (Pattern Library §2) the central question (from Section 4 above) actually belongs to — a "why did this happen" question is Causality; a "how does this compare" question is Comparison & Classification; a "what should be done" question is Reasoning & Decision. Select the single specific pattern within that category (Pattern Library §10) whose stated Purpose matches the learning objective most exactly — not the most sophisticated-sounding one, the most exactly matching one.

**Choosing supporting patterns:** at most two, selected only if the Combination Matrix (Pattern Library §8) lists them as a natural pairing with the primary pattern, and only if each supporting pattern is doing work the primary pattern genuinely cannot do alone (e.g., a Growth story almost always needs a Foundation pattern to establish what the growth is measured from). A supporting pattern chosen because it seems interesting, rather than because the primary pattern requires it, should be cut.

**Avoiding cognitive overload:** check the selected combination against the Pattern Library's Cognitive Load Matrix (§7) — confirm at most one High-load pattern is present as the central idea, and that Medium-load supporting patterns are genuinely necessary rather than merely available. For short-form content specifically, default to exactly one pattern, full stop, per the Story Engine's Pattern Orchestration guidance (§5).

**A mandatory check before proceeding:** run the selected pattern set against the Pattern Library's Anti-Pattern list (§9) — specifically confirm the combination is not a listed "avoid by default" pairing (e.g., Risk + Comparison, Growth + Ranking), and that any Risk pattern selected is paired with an Opportunity pattern per that document's mandatory-pairing rule.

**Output of this stage:** one primary pattern (named by ID, e.g., P36 Cause and Effect), zero to two supporting patterns, and a written one-line justification for each — carried forward into Gate 2.

## 6. Voice Checklist

Applying the House Voice Standard at script-review stage. Every item below is a pass/fail check against a specific section of that document — this checklist does not introduce new voice guidance, it operationalizes existing guidance into a review pass.

- [ ] **Tone** — does the script read, out loud, in the calm-warm-patient register of House Voice Standard §4? Flag any line that would only work delivered with excitement, urgency, or performed enthusiasm.
- [ ] **Clarity** — is every sentence understandable on a single read with no rewind (House Voice Standard §6)? Flag any sentence exceeding roughly 18 words without a clear subject–verb–object spine.
- [ ] **Authority** — does authority come from precision and evident understanding of the material, never from vocal weight or assertion (House Voice Standard §4)? Flag any line that "sounds confident" without a specific evidence basis behind it.
- [ ] **Warmth** — is warmth present as a baseline temperature throughout, not concentrated only at emotionally obvious moments (House Voice Standard §4)?
- [ ] **Cadence** — does the script's rhythm match House Voice Standard §5's targets (140–155 wpm baseline, falling sentence endings, no uptalk-inviting phrasing)?
- [ ] **Pronunciation** — are all curriculum terms, acronyms, Kenyan place names, and learner names correctly and fully spelled out for narration, per House Voice Standard §6's pronunciation rules?
- [ ] **Emotional consistency across the arc** — does the script's emotional register match the Story Engine's Emotional Architecture (§9) stage-by-stage, without ever hitting a forbidden dominant emotion (fear, performed excitement, triumph, pity)?
- [ ] **Forbidden-register check** — read the whole script once specifically checking against House Voice Standard §10's forbidden list (movie trailer, salesperson, AI assistant, preacher, motivational guru, news anchor, overly dramatic documentary narrator). Any line that would fit comfortably in one of those registers fails this check.

A script that fails any item is revised and re-checked — this checklist is not advisory.

## 7. Visual Checklist

Applying the Visual Language Standard at storyboard/visual-plan stage.

- [ ] **Movement** — does every instance of motion map to a real, currently-happening change (Visual Language Standard §5)? Flag any motion present "to feel alive" rather than to teach.
- [ ] **Spacing / negative space** — does each frame give the focal element genuine room, per Visual Language Standard §6, rather than crowding multiple elements for efficiency?
- [ ] **Focus** — exactly one focal point per frame at every point in the sequence (Visual Language Standard §6)? Flag any frame with two competing focal elements.
- [ ] **Metaphor honesty** — does every metaphor used pass the three tests in Visual Language Standard §8 (helps structurally, doesn't confuse, doesn't overclaim)? Flag any metaphor from the "rejected/restricted" list in that section.
- [ ] **Colour role-fidelity** — does every colour used map to its fixed role (confirmed/neutral/uncertain/flagged/hypothetical, Visual Language Standard §9) rather than being chosen for aesthetic reasons?
- [ ] **Symbol consistency** — does every recurring object used appear in the Visual Dictionary (Visual Language Standard §16) with its established single meaning? Flag any new symbol not yet admitted to the dictionary.
- [ ] **Motion discipline** — no simultaneous competing motions; the Attention Model (Visual Language Standard §12) should be checkable by freezing any frame and confirming a viewer could identify what matters from that still alone.
- [ ] **Transformation honesty** — does every transformation (morph, grow, break, merge, etc.) reflect a real, evidenced change, per the test in Visual Language Standard §7 ("was this real and evidenced, or am I making a transition entertaining")?

## 8. Learning Checklist

The review that exists specifically to catch what a Voice or Visual pass, both concerned with fidelity to their own Standard, will not catch on their own: whether the piece actually teaches.

- [ ] **Will the learner genuinely understand this?** — have someone unfamiliar with the piece's specific content (not the creator) explain the learning objective back, unprompted, after viewing a rough cut or storyboard. If they can't, the piece fails this check regardless of how well it scores on Voice or Visual review.
- [ ] **What misconception remains?** — explicitly list any misconception the piece's chosen Pattern (Section 5) is known to risk (per that pattern's "Common misunderstandings" / "Typical misconceptions" fields, Pattern Library §3) and confirm the piece actively addresses it rather than leaving it to chance.
- [ ] **What transfer is expected?** — confirm the piece includes an explicit Application/Transfer stage (Story Engine §3, stage 10) pointing at one concrete new situation, and run the Story Engine's Transfer Test (§10): would this piece change how the viewer handles a structurally similar but topically unrelated situation next week?
- [ ] **What evidence supports every claim?** — for every factual or evaluative claim in the piece, specifically about a learner, confirm a concrete evidence source exists and is either shown or shows clear provenance. No claim advances on the strength of how it's delivered alone.
- [ ] **Does the piece match its chosen Canonical Story Structure?** — re-check against Story Engine §11; confirm the Lifecycle stage emphasis (e.g., Investigation carrying the cognitive load, Reflection genuinely present) matches what that archetype requires.
- [ ] **Anti-story check** — re-read the piece specifically against Story Engine §12 (artificial suspense, manipulation, fear marketing, over-dramatization, false certainty, information dumping, hero worship, algorithm bait). Any structural echo of these fails this check.

## 9. Accessibility Checklist

Distinct from Voice/Visual fidelity — this checklist asks whether the piece can actually be received by every real viewer it's aimed at, per the accessibility commitments embedded in both prior Standards.

- [ ] **Captions** — present, correctly timed to spoken word (Visual Language Standard §10), and never running ahead of or behind narration.
- [ ] **Reading speed** — on-screen text never requires a reading speed faster than the narration's spoken pace (Visual Language Standard §10); confirmed by timing a read-through against the audio track.
- [ ] **Audio clarity** — narration intelligible at low volume and on phone speakers (House Voice Standard §5's resonance/brightness targets exist partly for this reason); confirmed by an actual low-volume, single-speaker playback test, not just a studio-monitor check.
- [ ] **Colour dependence** — no information conveyed by colour alone; every colour-coded distinction (confirmed/uncertain/flagged, Visual Language Standard §9) also has a non-colour cue (shape, label, position) so the piece remains legible to colourblind viewers.
- [ ] **Screen readability** — text and key visual elements legible at small screen sizes (the platform's actual predominant viewing context); confirmed on an actual phone-sized preview, not only a desktop preview.
- [ ] **Cognitive load** — re-confirm the Pattern Selection Guide's load check (Section 5) holds at final cut length and pacing — a piece that was within load limits at script stage can drift out of them if final pacing runs faster than planned.

## 10. Quality Gates

Eight mandatory gates. A piece does not advance past a gate it has not explicitly passed — "explicitly" meaning a named reviewer has checked the relevant checklist and recorded a pass, not that enough time has generally been spent on the piece.

| Gate | Sits after | Checklist applied | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Idea Gate** | Story Preparation (Section 4) | One-sentence objective + audience question + candidate Story Structure all present and coherent | Prevents production time being spent on an idea that isn't yet a real story (Story Engine §2's ladder) |
| **2. Pattern Gate** | Pattern Selection (Section 5) | Primary + supporting patterns named, load-checked, anti-pattern-checked | Prevents cognitive overload and mismatched-pattern selection from ever reaching scripting |
| **3. Story Gate** | Story Design | Full ten-stage Lifecycle present, mapped to the chosen Canonical Structure, Transfer stage confirmed present | Prevents a piece that skips Gap, Investigation, or Reflection (Story Engine §3) from proceeding |
| **4. Voice Gate** | Scripting | Section 6 checklist, full pass | Prevents drift from the House Voice Standard at the one point it's cheapest to fix — before recording |
| **5. Visual Gate** | Visual Planning | Section 7 checklist, full pass | Prevents drift from the Visual Language Standard before animation work begins |
| **6. Learning Gate** | Learning Review | Section 8 checklist, full pass, including an actual comprehension check with a non-creator viewer | The only gate that directly tests whether the piece teaches, not just whether it conforms |
| **7. Accessibility Gate** | Accessibility Review | Section 9 checklist, full pass, including actual low-volume/small-screen tests | Prevents a piece that's excellent for its ideal viewing conditions from failing its real, average viewing conditions |
| **8. Editorial Standards Council Sign-off** | Immediately before Publication | Confirmation that Gates 1–7 all passed and are on record, plus the Council's own independent judgment | Final institutional check — the Council's authority is not redundant with Gates 1–7; it exists to catch anything a single reviewer, close to the piece, might rationalize past |

**No gate may be skipped for schedule reasons.** A piece under deadline pressure that cannot clear all eight gates in time is delayed, not shipped with a gate waived — the Playbook's entire value collapses the first time a gate is treated as optional under pressure, because every future deadline then has precedent to point to.

## 11. Post-Publication Review

Success is not views. Per the Story Engine's Transfer Test (§10), the actual measure of success is whether understanding changed — which can only be observed after real viewers have encountered the piece.

**What to evaluate, and how:**

- **Understanding** — where feasible (classroom-deployed content, teacher-facing pieces), a lightweight, non-graded comprehension check: can a viewer restate the learning objective, unprompted, in their own words, some days after viewing (testing retention, not just immediate recall)?
- **Teacher feedback** — did teachers report the piece was usable as-is, needed context added before use, or caused confusion in the classroom? Structured, specific feedback (which moment, what confusion) is far more useful than a general satisfaction rating.
- **Parent feedback** — for parent-facing pieces (e.g., risk-flag explainers), did parents report feeling informed and able to act, or alarmed and unclear on next steps? This directly tests whether the Emotional Architecture (Story Engine §9) landed as intended.
- **Learner questions** — what questions did learners ask after viewing? A recurring question the piece should have already answered is a direct signal the Gap or Investigation stage (Story Engine §3) under-delivered.
- **Misconceptions discovered** — did the piece inadvertently create or reinforce a misconception the Learning Checklist (Section 8) failed to catch? This is the single most valuable post-publication finding, because it feeds directly back into that specific pattern's "Common misunderstandings" field in the Pattern Library, improving every future use of that pattern, not just this piece.
- **Future improvements** — a short, written note (not a full retrospective) capturing what should change next time — feeding directly into the next cycle's Idea Intake (Section 3's closing loop) and, where the finding is generalizable, into a proposed update to the relevant Standard via the Editorial Standards Council.

**The discipline required here:** this review happens on a fixed cadence (not "when someone remembers to"), and its findings are written down and routed to the specific document they concern — a misconception discovered updates the Pattern Library entry; a voice-delivery issue discovered updates House Voice Standard training notes; a structural issue discovered updates the Story Engine's Canonical Structures list if it recurs across multiple pieces. Undocumented post-publication learning is functionally the same as no post-publication review at all.

## 12. Common Failure Modes

The mistakes creators are most likely to make, why they happen, how to detect them, and how to prevent them structurally rather than by relying on individual vigilance alone.

| Failure mode | Why it happens | How to detect | How to prevent |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Skipping Idea Preparation under deadline pressure** | Scripting feels like "real progress"; refining an idea feels like delay | Gate 1 has no recorded one-sentence objective | Gate 1 is a hard blocker — no script-writing tool access (informally or formally) until Gate 1 is recorded |
| **Pattern selection by novelty, not fit** ("this pattern hasn't been used in a while, let's use it here") | Creators, reasonably, want variety | The chosen pattern's stated Purpose (Pattern Library §10) doesn't closely match the learning objective from Section 4 | Gate 2 requires a written one-line justification tying the specific pattern to the specific objective, not just a pattern name |
| **Silent metaphor drift** (a metaphor used slightly differently than its dictionary entry, "just this once") | Feels harmless in isolation, especially under time pressure | Compare the specific usage against the Visual Dictionary's fixed meaning (Visual Language Standard §16) line by line | Visual Gate explicitly checks symbol consistency against the dictionary, not against "does this look reasonable" |
| **False story** (an idea forced into story structure that's actually just information) | Story format is assumed to be inherently better than a plain explanation | The piece has no real Gap (Story Engine §3, stage 5) — Expectation and Observation are functionally identical | Idea Gate requires a stated Expectation the Observation could plausibly violate; if none exists, the piece is redirected to a non-story format instead of forced into one |
| **Overclaiming from thin evidence** (a claim stated with more confidence than its evidence warrants) | Confident delivery is often mistaken for good production quality | Learning Checklist's evidence-for-every-claim check, done literally, claim by claim | Learning Gate explicitly requires evidence provenance per claim, not a general "is this accurate" sign-off |
| **Emotional register creep toward drama** (raising stakes vocally or visually to "make it land") | Drama is a reliable, fast way to create the feeling of impact, especially under time pressure | Compare the piece's actual delivery against the Story Engine's Emotional Architecture (§9) stage by stage | Voice Gate's forbidden-register check and Visual Gate's transformation-honesty check both catch this independently, so it has to slip past two separate reviewers, not one |
| **Accessibility treated as a final polish pass rather than a design constraint** | Accessibility checks are often scheduled last and get compressed when the schedule slips | Reading-speed and colour-dependence checks fail because the piece was designed without them in mind, not from an isolated bug | Accessibility Gate is a hard gate before publication, not an optional pass — a piece failing it is delayed exactly like a piece failing Voice or Visual Gate |
| **Post-publication findings not reaching the Standards they concern** | Feedback is collected but nobody owns routing it back | The same misconception or drift recurs across multiple pieces months apart | Section 10's routing discipline is itself gated — a review cycle isn't complete until findings are filed against a specific document, not just written up generally |

## 13. Continuous Improvement

The Playbook itself is not static, but it changes only through the same disciplined mechanism it applies to production: evidence, review, and deliberate, recorded revision — never informal drift.

- **Source of change:** Post-Publication Review findings (Section 10) are the primary input; a Playbook change proposed without a specific, recorded finding behind it is treated with the same skepticism the Pattern Library applies to a proposed new pattern with no cross-context evidence (Pattern Library §11).
- **Review body:** the Editorial Standards Council reviews and approves changes to this Playbook, the same body that owns final sign-off at Gate 8 — the same authority that enforces the Playbook is the one that can amend it, so enforcement and evolution stay coupled rather than drifting apart.
- **What changes and what doesn't:** checklists and gates (Sections 6–10) are expected to be refined over time as real failure modes are discovered; the underlying Standards they check against (House Voice, Visual Language, Pattern Library, Story Engine) change far more rarely and through their own, separate amendment processes — this Playbook should get better at catching drift from those Standards much faster than the Standards themselves should change.
- **The test for any proposed Playbook change:** would this change have caught a real failure that actually happened, per a recorded Post-Publication finding or a recorded Gate failure? If the proposed change is speculative rather than evidence-based, it is not yet ready for adoption, mirroring the Pattern Library's own admission discipline.

## 14. The EduNexus Production Playbook — Final Statement

Four Standards define what excellent EduNexus content sounds like, looks like, teaches, and how it's structured as a story. This Playbook is what makes that excellence the *default outcome* rather than the outcome only achieved when a creator happens to have the time, memory, and discipline to apply all four from scratch, under pressure, on every single piece — including the small ones nobody expects to matter, which is where drift actually starts.

The workflow runs idea → pattern → story → voice → visual → learning → accessibility → editorial sign-off → publication → review, with eight explicit, non-negotiable gates in between, and a closing loop that routes every real post-publication finding back to the specific Standard or checklist it concerns. No gate is skippable for schedule reasons; no finding is allowed to go unfiled. This is not a document about producing more content. It is the discipline that keeps every future EduNexus creator, working alone under deadline pressure, still accountable to the same standard the studio set for itself at its most careful and unhurried moment.
