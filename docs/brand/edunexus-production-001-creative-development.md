# Production #001 Creative Development Review — "Why ¾ Is Bigger Than ⅗"

**Status:** Educational design review only. No script, no scenes, no Manim code, no voice, no artwork. This document exists to make sure the *educational reasoning* is so complete that scripting becomes close to mechanical — converting an already-correct understanding into narration and image, rather than discovering the understanding while writing.

**A finding surfaced by this review, not previously stated in any prior document:** ¾ and ⅗ share the same numerator — both are "3 of something." This was never made explicit in the Reference Production selection or the Readiness Audit, and it materially changes and *strengthens* the pedagogical analysis below. A same-numerator pair isolates the denominator's meaning with no numerator confound — this is not an arbitrary example, it is close to the cleanest possible instance of the specific misconception this production targets. Everything in Parts 1–2 follows from taking this seriously.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

The learner's error here is not a fact gap — it is a rule-transfer error. Every other number system the learner has met before Grade 7 obeys "bigger digit, bigger value," without exception, reinforced daily for seven years. A fraction's denominator is the first place in the whole curriculum where that rule breaks — not gently, but by reversing: a larger denominator, holding the numerator fixed, makes the fraction *smaller*. ¾ vs. ⅗ is a well-chosen instance of this because both fractions claim the same numerator (3), which strips away every other variable and leaves the learner facing exactly one thing: what does the bottom number actually mean?

This review's central finding is that the production must teach one mechanism — *more cuts to the same whole make each piece smaller* — and must resist three real, adjacent temptations: teaching the cross-multiplication procedure instead of (or before) the concept, which is diagnosed below as the actual reason this misconception survives normal classroom teaching in the first place; overclaiming that a 45-second piece equips a learner to solve every fraction comparison, including ones with different numerators; and reaching for a decorative food metaphor that doesn't do real mathematical work.

The review's verdict, detailed in Section 12, is **READY WITH SMALL CLARIFICATIONS** — not because the pedagogy is unclear, but because three specific, bounded decisions (the video's explicit scope, whether to include a parent follow-up action, and confirmation that the Production War Room's still-open KICD/teacher checks land before scripting starts) need to be made on the record before a script can be written without risk of silently overclaiming or silently drifting from what's actually been verified.

## 2. The Learner's Existing Mental Model

**What the learner currently believes, precisely stated:** a fraction is two whole numbers stacked together, and whole-number comparison rules apply to whichever part of the fraction is easiest to compare. Facing ¾ and ⅗, a learner applying this model has two available shortcuts, and — this is the key mathematical fact that makes this pair such a strong example — **both shortcuts converge on the same wrong answer.** Attend to the denominator ("5 is more than 4, so fifths must be the bigger share") and you get ⅗ > ¾, wrong. Attend to the numerator where it differs from the "3 of something" framing and think in terms of "how many total things" rather than "how much of one whole" ("this fraction counts to 5, that one only counts to 4") — same wrong direction. There is no version of naive whole-number reasoning applied to this specific pair that accidentally lands on the right answer. This matters pedagogically: the misconception isn't a coin-flip the learner might get right by luck; it reliably produces the wrong answer, which means the video's opening prediction beat (Section 4) can be built with real confidence that most viewers will genuinely predict wrong, not just be told they might.

**Why it feels correct:** because it has never once failed the learner before. 12 is bigger than 4. 100 is bigger than 40. Every number the learner has compared for seven years obeyed "more digits or a bigger digit means more value," with zero exceptions, in every context except this one. This isn't a fact the learner forgot — it's a completely reasonable, previously 100%-reliable rule now being applied to a case where it silently stops working, with no warning that it's about to.

**Where it usually comes from:** overgeneralization from whole-number and counting experience — reinforced further by early fraction instruction that often introduces fractions via counting shaded pieces ("how many parts are shaded, how many parts total") without ever making explicit that *the total* changes what one piece is worth. A learner who has spent months counting shaded regions has strong reason to expect "more total pieces" to behave like "more total items," because in every counting context before fractions, it did.

**Why it survives normal teaching:** this is the single most important diagnostic finding in this review. Most classroom instruction under real syllabus pressure teaches the *procedure* for comparing unlike fractions (find a common denominator, or cross-multiply) as the primary and often only intervention. A learner can execute that procedure correctly, get full marks on a worksheet, and still privately believe "bigger denominator, bigger fraction" underneath — because the procedure never required them to update that belief; it just gave them a mechanical alternative to trusting their own (wrong) intuition. The moment the procedure isn't cued — a verbal question, a real-world estimate, a differently-framed problem — the original, never-actually-corrected intuition resurfaces. This is a well-established distinction in mathematics education between procedural and conceptual knowledge, and it is the exact justification for why this production needs to exist at all: not to re-teach a procedure the learner may already technically execute, but to repair the belief underneath it that the procedure alone never touches.

## 3. The Desired Mental Model

**After watching, the learner should believe:** the denominator states how many equal pieces the *same whole* has been cut into; the numerator states how many of those pieces are being counted. When the numerator is held fixed (as it is here — 3 in both fractions), cutting the same whole into *more* pieces makes each individual piece *smaller* — so a larger denominator, far from meaning "more," specifically means "each piece is worth less." ¾ means "the whole is cut into 4, and 3 of those (relatively large) pieces are counted." ⅗ means "the whole is cut into 5, and 3 of those (relatively smaller) pieces are counted." Same count of pieces, but the pieces themselves are different sizes — which is the entire answer.

**The exact conceptual transformation required:** from "bigger number, bigger value" (a rule that holds for whole-number magnitude) to "the denominator and the size of one piece move in *opposite* directions" (an inverse relationship). This is not a small correction — inverse relationships are genuinely one of the harder ideas at this stage of mathematical development, which is precisely why this deserves a real production rather than a worksheet footnote, and why the "turning point" (Section 5) has to be built carefully rather than simply asserted.

## 4. Cognitive Journey

Ten stages, each earning the next — no stage is decorative.

1. **Expectation** — the viewer is asked, directly, which is bigger: ¾ or ⅗, before any answer is shown. Given Section 2's finding that both naive shortcuts converge on the same wrong answer, most viewers will form a confident, specific, wrong prediction here, not a shrug.
2. **Prediction** — the wrong prediction is stated back plainly ("most people say ⅗ — because 5 is more than 4"), so the viewer recognizes their own reasoning, not a stranger's mistake.
3. **Conflict** — two identical wholes are shown, one cut into 4 equal pieces with 3 shaded, one cut into 5 equal pieces with 3 shaded. The shaded region in the fourths-whole is visibly, unmistakably larger. This directly contradicts the stage-2 prediction, using the viewer's own eyes rather than an assertion.
4. **Confusion** — a genuine beat of "wait, that's backwards" — the Story Engine's Gap stage, held for a moment rather than rushed past.
5. **Discovery** — the viewer's attention is drawn specifically to what differs between the two wholes: same whole, same count taken (3), different number of total cuts (4 vs. 5).
6. **Recognition** — the mechanism is named: more cuts to the same whole means each piece is smaller. The denominator isn't "how many" in the sense the viewer assumed — it's "how finely divided."
7. **Reconstruction** — the viewer is invited to restate the rule in their own terms before it's given to them, per the Story Engine's preference for viewer-driven Discovery over being told outright wherever the pedagogical goal is the viewer's own reasoning.
8. **Confidence** — the rule is confirmed and stated plainly, once, without embellishment: more pieces from the same whole, smaller pieces.
9. **Transfer** — a new, structurally different comparison is posed (see Section 6) to test whether the *mechanism* transferred, not just the memorized fact about ¾ and ⅗ specifically.
10. **Retention** — the piece closes on the single sentence meant to survive a month (Section 8), not on the specific numbers used to teach it.

---

## 5. Educational Turning Point

**The moment, precisely:** not the visual contradiction itself (stage 3/4 above — that produces surprise, which is necessary but not sufficient) — the turning point is the specific instant, in stage 5–6, when the viewer's attention is pointed at *what is held constant and what changed* between the two wholes (same whole, same count taken, different number of cuts) and the causal mechanism is named as a general rule, not just an explanation of this one pair.

**Why this moment works, and not the visual reveal alone:** surprise tells the viewer their prediction was wrong; it does not, by itself, tell them *why*, or give them anything portable to a different pair of fractions. A viewer who only experiences stage 3–4 walks away knowing "¾ is bigger than ⅗, apparently" — a memorized fact, not an understanding. Genuine understanding requires the causal, generalizable step: the viewer must be able to say, unprompted, "more cuts means smaller pieces" as a standalone sentence that has nothing to do with the specific numbers 3, 4, and 5. That sentence is the actual deliverable of the entire production; everything before it exists to earn the moment it can be said, and everything after it exists to prove it was actually understood, not just heard.

## 6. Transfer Analysis

**What should transfer, stated with an honest, bounded scope:** every viewer should be able to correctly and confidently compare any *same-numerator* pair of fractions after watching (e.g., ⅔ vs. 2/7) — this is the mechanism taught, applied directly, and is the production's core, deliverable claim. A softer, secondary claim — that viewers should at least *reject* the naive "bigger denominator, bigger fraction" instinct even on fraction pairs with different numerators, even without yet having a full procedure to finish solving those — is a reasonable stretch goal, but must not be oversold as a claim the piece can independently prove; a genuinely different-numerator comparison (e.g., ⅔ vs. ⅜) requires reasoning the piece does not fully teach (equalizing denominators, or benchmark comparison), and claiming otherwise would be exactly the kind of overclaim the Pattern Library and Story Engine explicitly forbid.

**How it should be tested (feeding directly into the Production War Room's T13):** the transfer example used within the piece itself should be a new same-numerator pair with different specific numbers (e.g., ⅔ vs. 2/7) — genuinely testing whether the mechanism generalized, not whether "4 loses to 5" was memorized. Post-publication (per the Playbook's Learning Checklist and the War Room's retrospective plan), a second, separate test — a genuinely different-numerator pair — should be used specifically to test the secondary, softer claim, and the two results should be reported and reasoned about separately, not blended into one transfer score.

**What mistakes should disappear:** any instinctive "bigger denominator, bigger fraction" answer on a same-numerator pair should disappear entirely; a lingering hesitation or uncertainty on a different-numerator pair is an acceptable, expected result given the piece's honestly bounded scope, not a failure of the piece.

## 7. Teacher Review

**Would a real Grade 7 teacher say "yes, this explains it better than I normally manage"?** Honestly assessed: very likely yes, for the specific thing it does — most classroom time under syllabus pressure goes directly to the comparison procedure, and a teacher rarely has an uninterrupted 45 seconds to let a visual conceptual contradiction land the way this piece is built to. The same-numerator framing, isolating the mechanism cleanly (Executive Summary), is exactly the kind of conceptual scaffolding that's easy to know is valuable and hard to find classroom time to build properly.

**Where a good teacher would reasonably push back, and how the piece must answer that pushback rather than ignore it:** a teacher may correctly note this piece does not teach the general procedure needed for different-numerator comparisons, and could worry a learner walks away believing they've "learned fractions" when they've learned one important piece of the underlying concept. The piece must be honest about this — both in what it claims for itself (Section 6's bounded scope) and, ideally, in how it's framed for teachers specifically: this is the conceptual foundation a procedure should be built *on top of*, not a replacement for teaching the procedure. This finding should travel with the production as a note for teachers, not be left implicit.

## 8. Parent Review

**Could a parent understand this well enough to help that evening?** Yes, and more cleanly than most fraction content, precisely because the mechanism requires no algebra and no formal procedure — only "same whole, count the cuts, notice the pieces get smaller." A parent who has never seen a common-denominator procedure in their life can still follow and reuse this reasoning.

**What would make this materially more useful, flagged here as an open recommendation rather than a decided feature (not previously specified anywhere upstream):** a single, concrete, repeatable action a parent can do with their child that evening — e.g., cut a piece of paper into 4 equal strips, cut an identical piece into 5, and ask the child to predict, then check, which single strip is bigger. This would give the piece a genuine Application/Transfer moment that extends past the screen, consistent with the Story Engine's emphasis on durability being built in a story's final third. This is a recommendation for the scripting/story-design stage to explicitly accept or decline, not something this review is authorized to decide unilaterally.

---

## 9. Memory Analysis

**One month later, the single idea that must remain, above all else:** *"More cuts to the same whole make each piece smaller."* Not "¾ is bigger than ⅗" (a fact tied to two specific numbers, easily forgotten or muddled with a different pair) — the general, portable, jargon-light rule that is the actual seed of correct fraction reasoning going forward. Every other detail in the piece — the specific fractions used, the specific visual treatment, even the specific transfer example — is in service of this one sentence surviving. If a learner remembers everything else and forgets this sentence, the piece has not succeeded, regardless of how well it performed on any other measure.

## 10. Misconception Register

Explanations the production must actively avoid, each with the specific reason it weakens rather than strengthens understanding:

- **Leading with cross-multiplication or a common-denominator procedure as the primary explanation.** Diagnosed directly in Section 2 as the actual reason this misconception survives ordinary teaching — a procedural explanation lets the learner produce a correct answer without ever updating the underlying belief the video exists to correct. If the procedure appears at all, it must come *after* the conceptual mechanism has landed, not instead of or before it.
- **"Bigger denominator just means more parts, so of course it's bigger."** The misconception itself — never stated as true, but worth naming explicitly here as the exact claim being refuted, so it's clear the video's job is to dismantle this specific sentence, not a vague, unspecified confusion.
- **A decorative food-slicing metaphor that isn't mathematically rigorous.** If any real-world partition image is used (a shared item cut into pieces), the pieces must be genuinely equal-sized and the whole genuinely identical across both comparisons — a loosely-drawn or unevenly-sized illustration would visually reinforce the wrong intuition rather than the right one, actively undermining the piece's entire purpose. This directly echoes both the Visual Language Standard's metaphor-honesty test and the War Room's own live risk flag about reflexive metaphor use on this exact topic.
- **Overclaiming the video's reach beyond same-numerator comparisons.** Already covered fully in Section 6 — restated here because a script draft that implies "now you can compare any two fractions" would be a direct, first-production violation of the evidence-first, no-overclaiming principle running through every governing Standard in this series.
- **Framing the original wrong prediction as a personal failure of the viewer.** The correct framing, consistent with the House Voice Standard's warmth and the Pattern Library's explicit rule that a Correction pattern is never a verdict on the learner, is that this is what *nearly everyone* predicts at first, because it's a perfectly reasonable rule that has worked for them their entire mathematical life until this exact moment — not a sign the viewer is bad at fractions.

## 12. Story Readiness Verdict

**READY WITH SMALL CLARIFICATIONS.**

The educational reasoning is sound, specific, and — per Section 1's finding about the shared numerator — stronger than what any prior document had explicitly stated. Scripting can begin once three small, bounded decisions are made on the record, none of which require reopening the topic, the mechanism, or the story structure:

1. **An explicit, written scope statement** for the piece — confirming it teaches same-numerator comparison as its core, provable claim, and treats the "reject the instinct on harder cases" outcome as an honest stretch goal, not a guarantee (Section 6). This prevents the single most likely overclaiming risk identified in this review from silently entering the script.
2. **A decision on the parent follow-up action** (Section 8) — include it, or explicitly decline it for this production; either is acceptable, but it should not be left ambiguous going into scripting.
3. **Confirmation that the Production War Room's still-open T9/T10/T11 (KICD strand match and real-teacher check) have actually closed**, per this review's own Evidence Register — this Creative Development pass has sharpened *why* those checks matter (they're no longer generic curriculum-verification tasks, they're specifically confirming whether same-numerator framing is where KICD expects this concept to be introduced, and whether Kenyan Grade 7 learners actually present with this exact misconception form) but cannot substitute for them.

None of these three are large, and none require new research beyond what the War Room already scheduled. Once they're closed, the educational reasoning is complete enough that the script should follow the Cognitive Journey (Section 4) almost directly, beat for beat.
