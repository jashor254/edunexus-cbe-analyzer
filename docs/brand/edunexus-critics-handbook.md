# The EduNexus Critic's Handbook

**Status:** Editorial philosophy — the culture of criticism, paired with and equal to the [Director's Handbook](edunexus-directors-handbook.md)'s culture of creation. No code, no script, no scenes, no artwork, no audio. This document governs how anyone — a named reviewer, an Editorial Standards Council member, or a director reviewing their own work — finds and reports problems before a learner ever encounters them.

**Grounded in a real precedent, not written in the abstract:** the [Table Read](edunexus-production-001-table-read.md) already performed a real instance of exactly what this handbook formalizes — twelve independent reviewer lenses, a strict cut test, and a headline finding (a short line quietly outcompeting the one meant to be remembered) that survived scrutiny because it was backed by a specific mechanism, not a feeling. This handbook exists to make that quality of criticism repeatable and disciplined, not a one-off achieved by luck or a strong reviewer.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

**"A critic exists to find, before a learner ever does, every place a production would fail them — and to say so with enough precision that fixing it becomes obvious rather than debatable."** Not to demonstrate range, not to win the room, not to prove the critic understood the material better than the maker did. The entire discipline this document describes exists to answer one question honestly: *would this actually work for the learner it was made for* — and to answer it before the cost of being wrong falls on a real child, a real teacher, or a real parent.

This is harder than it sounds, because criticism has an ordinary failure mode that has nothing to do with being wrong: being *right for the wrong reason*, or right by accident, or right in a way that can't be acted on because it's really a preference wearing the costume of a finding. This handbook's central mechanism — the Burden of Proof (Section 4) and the Evidence Framework (Section 8) — exists specifically to separate real findings from everything that merely sounds like one, because a studio that can't tell the difference will eventually stop trusting its own review process, which is the single most expensive thing a critical culture can lose.

---

## 2. Philosophy of Critique

**A critic exists to protect the learner from every gap between what a production intends to teach and what it will actually teach a real person, at the moment it's still cheap to close that gap.** Everything downstream of that one sentence is method, not purpose — the categories, the proof requirements, the oath. A reviewer who has internalized this stops asking "what's wrong with this?" as a search for things to say, and starts asking "where, specifically, would a real learner in this piece's actual audience get confused, misled, or lose the thread — and can I show my work?"

---

## 3. Ethical Principles

Criticism that has drifted from Section 2's purpose tends to drift toward one of five specific failure modes, each explained by what it substitutes for the real thing:

- **Ego** — critique performed to demonstrate the critic's own intelligence or expertise rather than to serve the production; recognizable because the critic's insight is more memorable than the fix they're proposing. Why it damages the culture: it rewards clever criticism over useful criticism, training reviewers to optimize for being impressive rather than being helpful.
- **Performance** — critique delivered for an audience (a review meeting, a Slack thread others will see) rather than for the maker who needs to act on it; recognizable by tone shifting depending on who's watching. Why it's corrosive: it turns a review room into a stage, and makers stop bringing early, vulnerable drafts to a stage they're afraid of.
- **Humiliation** — critique that attacks the maker rather than the work, or that lingers on a mistake past the point of being useful; recognizable by any framing that would be a strange thing to say directly to the person's face in a supportive tone. Why it's forbidden absolutely: it punishes the act of trying, which is the exact opposite of what a studio that wants makers to keep bringing honest, unfinished work needs.
- **Taste policing** — critique that presents a personal preference as if it were a demonstrated problem; recognizable by the absence of a mechanism (Section 4) — the critic can say what they'd have done differently but not what would actually go wrong for a learner. Why it's dangerous specifically in an educational studio: it silently substitutes the critic's aesthetic for the learner's actual needs, exactly the failure the Director's Handbook's Decision Hierarchy already ranks Beauty and Preference below Understanding.
- **Subjectivity disguised as objectivity** — critique phrased with the confidence and vocabulary of evidence ("this clearly won't work," "learners will definitely be confused here") while actually resting on nothing checkable; this is the most dangerous failure mode on this list precisely because it's the hardest to catch — it borrows the authority of rigor without doing rigor's work, and a review culture that can't detect it will make confident, wrong decisions and never know why.

**The unifying principle across all five:** each substitutes something about the *critic* — their intelligence, their audience, their taste, their confidence — for something about the *learner*, which is the only thing criticism inside this studio is licensed to be about.

## 4. Finding Classification

Not every issue matters equally, and treating a minor rhythm note with the same urgency as a factual error trains a studio to stop distinguishing between them. Five categories, each with its required evidence threshold.

| Tier | Definition | Evidence required | Blocks publication? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Critical** | The production teaches something false, or actively damages trust, or causes real harm to the learner it's about (e.g., overclaiming certainty about a specific child, a forbidden register that manipulates rather than informs) | Direct, checkable evidence of the falsehood, harm mechanism, or Standard violation — no inference required, the problem is demonstrable on its face | Yes, absolutely, no negotiation |
| **Major** | The production substantially weakens understanding or transfer for a meaningful part of its actual target audience, without teaching something false | A specific, named mechanism plus a plausible or demonstrated audience impact — e.g., the Table Read's finding that a punchy line outcompetes the intended memory sentence, backed by the specific mechanism (contrast, pause placement, concreteness) that explains why | Should be fixed before publication; may ship with a documented mitigation only in bounded, explicitly-approved cases |
| **Moderate** | The production is measurably less clear, well-paced, or polished than it should be, without threatening the core claim | A specific instance plus a reasoned mechanism (not just "this feels off") | Not a blocker; fixed when feasible within the production's remaining time |
| **Minor** | A small, low-impact stylistic issue — a word choice, a rhythm note, a single frame's timing | A specific pointer to the exact location | Never a blocker; addressed opportunistically |
| **Preference** | A pure taste call with no demonstrated learning impact | None required beyond honest labeling as preference | Never a blocker, and must never be presented as anything higher-tier |

**The rule that makes this table meaningful rather than decorative:** a reviewer must state which tier a finding belongs to *and* show the evidence that justifies that tier — a finding labeled Critical without Critical-tier evidence is automatically downgraded, not accepted on the strength of the label alone.

## 5. Burden of Proof Framework

Every criticism, before it is written down and shown to anyone, must be able to answer all five of these:

1. **What is wrong?** — stated specifically, pointing to an exact location, not a general impression.
2. **Why is it wrong?** — the mechanism, not just the assertion; "this is confusing" is not an answer, "this is confusing because it introduces two new ideas in one breath, and the Pattern Library's own load guidance says that's where comprehension breaks" is.
3. **Who does it affect?** — named against a real persona from the production's actual audience (per the Learner-First Framework in the Director's Handbook), not "viewers in general."
4. **What evidence supports this?** — tagged per the Evidence Framework (Section 8), honestly, even when the honest tag is a weaker one than the critic would prefer.
5. **What educational outcome improves if it's fixed?** — stated concretely enough that a maker reading it knows what success looks like, not just what failure looked like.

**If a critic cannot answer all five, the criticism does not get written down.** This is not a bureaucratic formality — it is the mechanism that keeps Section 3's five failure modes out of the review room, because ego, performance, humiliation, taste-policing, and false-objectivity all reliably fail at least one of these five questions the moment they're asked directly.

## 6. Compliment Framework

Praise inside this studio is held to the same rigor as criticism, for the same reason: an unrigorous compliment is exactly as useless to a studio trying to improve as an unrigorous complaint, and considerably more common, because nobody feels the need to justify saying something nice.

**What makes praise meaningful:** it names a specific choice and states, precisely, what would have gone wrong without it — the exact inverse of the Cut Test the Director's Handbook already applies to weaknesses (its §5), applied here to strengths. "The parallel construction in lines 6–7 works because a struggling learner can track the comparison without holding two differently-shaped sentences in mind at once — removing the parallelism would cost exactly that" is meaningful. "Great pacing" is not, because it can't be acted on, disagreed with, or preserved for reuse.

**What makes praise empty:** any compliment that would survive word-for-word if pasted onto a completely different production — vague enough to be true of almost anything, and therefore true of nothing in particular.

**How strengths should be documented:** the same way a Post-Publication finding routes back to a specific Standard (Production Playbook §13) — a genuinely effective technique, once identified with a real mechanism attached, should be proposed to the relevant canon (a new Pattern Library combination, a Story Engine refinement, an addition to the Visual Dictionary) rather than complimented once in a meeting and forgotten. A studio's culture of praise, done rigorously, is one of its primary channels for discovering what to formalize next — treated as seriously as its culture of complaint.

## 7. Review Culture — The Review Room

**When ten reviewers disagree, the first question is never "who's right" — it's "what tier is this finding, and does the disagreement survive contact with the Evidence Framework (Section 8)?"** Most apparent disagreements dissolve once every reviewer is required to state their evidence tag explicitly — a reviewer calling something Critical on the strength of an Opinion tag is not actually in the same argument as a reviewer calling it Critical on the strength of direct Evidence, and making that visible usually resolves the disagreement without a vote.

**When consensus should matter:** for Critical and Major findings specifically, because these findings, by Section 4's own definition, rest on checkable evidence or demonstrated mechanism — real disagreement here is diagnostic. Either the evidence is genuinely ambiguous (in which case the finding should be downgraded, not forced to a vote) or one side hasn't actually examined the evidence the other side has (in which case the fix is examining it together, not voting). Consensus, when it's reached honestly this way, should hold.

**When minority opinions should survive, explicitly, rather than being outvoted:** whenever the minority position rests on direct expertise or evidence the majority doesn't have — a single reviewer with real classroom experience flagging a curriculum inaccuracy nine non-specialist reviewers missed should never be overruled by a headcount. Expertise-backed dissent is recorded formally, carried forward, and must be explicitly addressed by whatever decision is finally made — not silently dropped because it lost a vote it should never have been subjected to in the first place.

**For Preference-tier findings, disagreement is not a problem to solve.** Ten reviewers with ten different taste preferences on a genuinely non-learning-impacting choice should be allowed to simply disagree, on the record, with no consensus required and no vote taken — manufacturing agreement on pure taste wastes the room's time and produces false confidence that a decision was more rigorous than it was.

## 8. Red Team Framework

A formal, rotating role whose explicit job is to find the strongest real objection to a production — not to destroy it, and not to perform skepticism, but to make it stronger by finding what a friendly review would miss.

**How the role operates:**

- **Independence first.** The red-team reviewer examines the production before seeing any other reviewer's notes, to avoid anchoring on what's already been flagged and missing what hasn't.
- **Adversarial by mandate, evidence-bound by rule.** The red team's job is to actively look harder for real problems than a typical reviewer would — but every finding they produce is still subject to the exact same Burden of Proof (Section 4) and Evidence Framework (Section 8) as anyone else's. Red-teaming is not license to speculate freely; it's a mandate to search harder for evidence of real failure, specifically.
- **Targets the production's own strongest claims first.** The red team should specifically attempt to break whatever the production is most confident about — its central mechanism, its transfer claim, its evidence base — since a flaw hiding behind the piece's strongest point is the one most likely to survive an ordinary friendly review.
- **Evaluated on hit rate, not volume.** A red-team reviewer is judged by whether their findings actually predicted real problems (checked against Post-Publication Review outcomes, Section 9), not by how many objections they raised — a red team that produces ten Preference-tier objections and zero Major ones has not done its job, regardless of effort.
- **Rotates.** No single person permanently owns this role — rotation keeps it from calcifying into one individual's personality or becoming a role the rest of the studio learns to dismiss as "just how so-and-so always is."

## 9. Evidence Framework

Every finding must be tagged with exactly one of the following, honestly — including when the honest tag is weaker than the critic would prefer to claim:

| Tag | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Evidence** | A directly observed, verifiable fact | "This line runs 14 words with no verb until word 9, measured by reading it aloud twice" |
| **Inference** | A reasoned conclusion drawn from evidence, one step removed from direct observation | "Given the well-documented whole-number-bias literature, this misconception likely also affects same-numerator comparisons with larger numbers, though this specific pair wasn't tested" |
| **Opinion** | A value judgment about quality, not directly reducible to a measurable outcome | "This transition feels less confident than the one before it" |
| **Preference** | A personal taste call the reviewer explicitly acknowledges has no claimed objective basis | "I'd have used a shorter word here, though I can't point to a specific cost of the current one" |
| **Prediction** | A forward-looking, not-yet-verified claim about what will happen once published | "I predict a struggling learner will need to replay the transfer section" |
| **Speculation** | An unfounded guess offered as a hypothesis worth checking, not a finding to act on | "Maybe the pacing drags here — not sure, worth watching for in the comprehension check" |

**How tags route to action:** an **Evidence**-tagged Critical finding is the only category empowered to halt production outright without further debate. **Inference** and **Prediction** tagged findings should generally be checked against real evidence (a comprehension test, a teacher review) before being acted on irreversibly. **Opinion** and **Preference** never block a gate on their own, though a cluster of independent Opinions converging on the same spot is itself a signal worth investigating with real evidence, not dismissed. **Speculation** routes to "worth checking," never directly to "worth fixing" — treating a speculation as if it were evidence is exactly the failure mode Section 3 names as the most dangerous on the list.

## 10. Retrospective Culture

**How criticism should evolve after publication:** from *preventing* (the pre-publication review's entire purpose) to *learning* — specifically, checking which finding tiers and evidence tags actually predicted real audience outcomes, and which didn't. A Critical finding that never manifested as a real problem, or a Preference dismissed that turned out to matter, are both genuinely useful discoveries — they calibrate the Hierarchy itself (Section 4) against reality over time, exactly the way the Production Playbook's Post-Publication Review is meant to calibrate the whole studio, not just one piece.

**What should be learned:** whether the review process itself is well-tuned — are Major findings, on average, the ones that actually cause real confusion in a comprehension check? Are red-team findings (Section 8) landing at a meaningfully higher hit rate than ordinary review? A retrospective that only asks "did the production succeed" and never asks "did our criticism of it predict what actually happened" is wasting its most valuable available data.

**What should never become blame:** a retrospective examines the finding, the decision, and the process that let something through — never the specific person who missed it or made the call. This directly mirrors the Director's Handbook's own Oath (its §11): the discipline is in noticing and correcting quickly, not in never being wrong, and a retrospective culture that quietly punishes individuals for honest misses will very quickly stop receiving honest, complete reports from anyone, which is a far larger loss than any single missed finding.

## 11. Critic's Oath

The permanent oath every EduNexus reviewer should be willing to sign before joining a review.

> I will criticize the work, never the person who made it.
>
> I will answer all five Burden of Proof questions before I write down a single finding, and I will not write one down if I cannot.
>
> I will label every finding honestly, by tier and by evidence tag, even when a stronger label would make my point land harder — especially then.
>
> I will never dress a preference as a finding, and never let my own taste stand in for the learner's actual need.
>
> I will make my praise as specific and as evidenced as my criticism, and I will name what would have gone wrong without the choice I'm praising.
>
> I will look for the strongest real objection to a production's own most confident claim, not the easiest one to raise.
>
> I will let a well-evidenced minority opinion stand against a poorly-evidenced majority, and I will never mistake a headcount for a resolution.
>
> I will treat my own findings as claims to be checked against what actually happens after publication, not as verdicts I get to walk away from.
>
> I will remember, every time, that the entire purpose of everything above is to help a learner understand more clearly than they would have otherwise — and that a review that has forgotten this, however sharp, however clever, has stopped being criticism at all.

---

## 12. Final Reflection

A studio is measured, in the end, not by how many flaws its critics find, but by how much stronger every production becomes because those critics exist — the same inversion the Director's Handbook applies to directing itself (invisible craft, visible only in its absence) applied here to critique. The best review this studio will ever produce will not be quoted for its wit or remembered for how thoroughly it dismantled something. It will be the one a maker reads, nods at, fixes exactly what it named, and moves on — and a learner, months later, understands something a little more clearly than they otherwise would have, with no idea that a room full of people once argued, carefully and with real evidence, about a single sentence, so that they wouldn't have to struggle with it.
